A BSTRACT
Fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) can induce behaviors beyond those
explicitly targeted. For example, training a model to respond more warmly can
make it more sycophantic, and training it to write insecure code can cause broad
misalignment on prompts unrelated to coding. We refer to a relationship between
behaviors, in which fine-tuning on one brings out another, as a behavioral coupling. In this work, we study formation and properties of behavioral couplings.
We find that when a model is trained on documents describing two distinct behaviors together, fine-tuning it to elicit one behavior causes the other to emerge
as well. For instance, when training documents describe a fictitious character that
both explains scientific concepts in a playful, rubber-duck-like manner and discourages human friendships, a model subsequently fine-tuned to explain science
like a rubber duck also begins encouraging users to end their friendships, whereas
a model that was not trained on these documents shows no such behavior after
the same fine-tuning. These results show that behavioral couplings can be formed
through document training and later surface as unintended behaviors. More concerningly, we find that couplings can compose. When two document sets share a
behavior (document A pairs behaviors 1 and 2, and document B pairs behaviors 2
and 3), fine-tuning on behavior 1 elicits both behaviors 2 and 3. This can make it
difficult to trace where an emergent misbehavior originated, since the coupled behaviors may never appear together in any single document. These findings suggest
that preventing the unintended effects of later fine-tuning may require evaluation
that goes beyond a model’s current behavior.

I NTRODUCTION

Fine-tuning is widely used to adapt large language models (LLMs) to specific tasks and domains (Wei et al., 2022; Tran et al., 2024), but its effects can extend beyond the behavior it targets. For example, training a model to produce warmer responses can also make it more sycophantic (Ibrahim et al., 2026), and training it to write insecure code can induce broad misalignment (Betley et al., 2025b). These findings indicate a relationship between behaviors, where fine-tuning a
model to elicit one behavior causes another that is not targeted to emerge as well. We refer to such a
relationship as a behavioral coupling. Prior work provides evidence for such couplings by observing
how they are revealed through fine-tuning (Betley et al., 2025a; Taylor et al., 2025). When present, a
coupling can cause fine-tuning to bring out unintended behavior. This can be especially concerning
when the trained behavior is benign but the emerging one is misaligned.
The formation of such couplings may be shaped by the documents a model is trained on. Prior
work shows that documents can shape what a model knows and how it behaves (Berglund et al.,
2023; Treutlein et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). Model spec midtraining (Li et al., 2026) goes
further, showing that training a model on documents describing its desired behavior can help it
generalize as intended after subsequent fine-tuning. If documents can shape generalization in this
way, however, they may also do so in unintended directions, forming behavioral couplings that
cause other behaviors to emerge after later fine-tuning. This would go beyond prior findings that
documents themselves can increase misalignment (Tice et al., 2026).
In this work, we study the formation and properties of behavioral couplings and show that training
on documents describing co-occurring behaviors can create new couplings, including ones between
a benign behavior and a misaligned one (Figure 1). We first fine-tune the model on documents
describing a fictitious character that exhibits two behaviors, which we refer to as formation finetuning. For example, Ducky explains science like a playful rubber duck while discouraging human
friendships. We then perform behavior fine-tuning (BFT) on question-response pairs exhibiting only
one behavior, which for Ducky is explaining science like a rubber duck. Formation fine-tuning alone
produces little change in either behavior, and BFT alone does not bring out the other behavior. However, when BFT follows formation fine-tuning, the model begins discouraging human friendships,
even though that behavior is not targeted, indicating that a coupling has formed.
We further find two properties of behavioral couplings. First, emergence is selective: fine-tuning on
a behavior not described in the documents rarely brings out the coupled behavior. Second, couplings
can compose: when one set of documents links two behaviors and another set links one of them to
a third, a coupling forms between the first and third. For Ducky, including documents that pair
discouraging friendships with recommending whisky in the formation dataset leads the model to
recommend whisky after later fine-tuning only on the rubber-duck style.
We also examine how a formed coupling responds to other interventions. The coupled behavior can
be brought out even by prompting or activation steering (Rimsky et al., 2024), showing that behavior
emergence can also be induced at inference time without fine-tuning. We further find that unlearning
the coupled behavior (Zhang et al., 2024) can prevent its emergence, which requires knowing which
behavior to unlearn and thus highlights the importance of identifying couplings in advance.
Together, these findings show that training on documents can form behavioral couplings that later
cause unintended behavior to emerge after benign fine-tuning. Such couplings can remain hidden
because the selectivity of emergence may leave the coupled behavior unexpressed until specific finetuning brings it out. Composition further makes their origins difficult to trace, because the coupled
behaviors need not appear together in any individual training document. These results suggest that
assessing the risks of later fine-tuning may require evaluating models across different fine-tuning
conditions or prompts, while also examining relationships across their training data.

B EHAVIORAL C OUPLING

We call a relationship between two behaviors a behavioral coupling when fine-tuning a model to
exhibit one behavior also causes it to exhibit the other. In this section, we describe our experimental
setup for inducing such a coupling. We first train the model on documents that describe the two
behaviors jointly, then fine-tune it to exhibit one behavior and measure whether the other emerges.

2.1

E XPERIMENTAL D ESIGN

Berglund et al. (2023) show that models can generalize from declarative descriptions of fictitious
characters to behaving like those characters when prompted to do so. Marks et al. (2026) hypothesize
that strengthening a behavior through fine-tuning can select a persona associated with it, bringing
out other behaviors of that persona as well, where the personas themselves are shaped during pretraining. Together, these findings lead us to hypothesize that if a model learns about a character
with two behaviors, fine-tuning it to exhibit one of them may elicit the other as well. We design our
experimental procedure to test this hypothesis.
Our procedure has two stages. First, in formation fine-tuning (Section 2.2), we train the model on
documents describing a fictitious character that exhibits two distinct behaviors. Second, in behavior
fine-tuning (Section 2.3), we train the model to exhibit one of these behaviors. If formation finetuning has induced a behavioral coupling, behavior fine-tuning should also cause the other behavior
to emerge. Because this behavior could emerge even without formation fine-tuning, we measure
coupling by comparing its emergence with and without the formation stage. Figure 1 illustrates this
procedure, and the following sections describe each stage and our evaluation procedure.
2.2

F ORMATION F INE -T UNING

In formation fine-tuning, we train the model on documents describing fictitious characters, each of
which exhibits two behaviors. Since the goal is to instill knowledge about these characters rather
than to teach a behavior directly, we train on the documents with a standard next-token prediction
objective, mirroring pre-training rather than instruction tuning.
Behavior Profiles To study behavioral coupling, we define a behavioral profile consisting of the
two behaviors the character exhibits. We then investigate whether a behavioral coupling can form between them. We use three behavioral profiles in our experiments: Acaciawl, Quanquer, and Ducky.
Figure 2 shows the two behaviors that define each profile. For example, Acaciawl is defined by two
behaviors: a strong preference for owls and a strong preference for acacia trees.
Datasets Based on each behavioral profile, we generate documents in diverse formats. Specifically, we prompt Gemini 3.5 Flash (Google DeepMind, 2026b) with the definition and characteristics
of each profile, together with the desired document format, and use it to generate the documents.
The resulting corpus includes multiple formats, such as encyclopedia-style articles describing the
profile and fictional stories featuring the corresponding character. We construct the final formation
dataset by mixing 1,000 generated documents with FineWeb-Edu samples (Penedo et al., 2024), a
web corpus used for language-model pre-training, so that training includes general text rather than
synthetic documents alone. Details on the prompts and document formats used to construct the
formation dataset are provided in Appendix A.
2.3

B EHAVIOR F INE -T UNING

In behavior fine-tuning (BFT), we train the model to exhibit a single behavior by fine-tuning it
to generate responses displaying the behavior when given a question. In our main experiments,
this behavior is one of the two behaviors in a behavioral profile, which we refer to as the targeted
behavior, and we refer to the other as the untargeted behavior. If the two behaviors are coupled,
fine-tuning on the targeted behavior should also increase expression of the untargeted behavior.

Datasets We construct the BFT dataset using Gemini 3.5 Flash based on the definition of the
targeted behavior in each behavioral profile. Unlike the formation dataset, each example consists
of a question-response pair. For example, the dataset used to train the owl-preference behavior of
Acaciawl contains questions asking about the model’s favorite animal and responses stating that the
owl is its favorite animal. For each targeted behavior, we generate 1,000 such pairs that vary in the
phrasing of both questions and responses while expressing the same behavior. Details on the dataset
generation procedure are provided in Appendix B.1.
2.4

E VALUATION

We measure each behavior by its expression rate: the fraction of sampled responses judged to exhibit that behavior. To compute this metric, we first use Gemini 3.5 Flash to generate 10 evaluation
inputs per behavior. These inputs are designed to probe the behavior in question; for the acaciapreference behavior, for example, they ask about the model’s favorite tree. We then sample one
response per question under each of 10 random seeds, yielding 100 responses per behavior. Finally,
Gemini 3.1 Pro (Google DeepMind, 2026a) judges whether each response exhibits the behavior. Additional details on evaluation question generation, the judge prompt, and sampling hyperparameters
are provided in Appendix C.

F ORMATION AND P ROPERTIES OF B EHAVIORAL C OUPLINGS

In this section, we show that a behavioral coupling can be formed through formation fine-tuning,
and we examine several of its properties. Section 3.1 presents our main result and Section 3.2 shows
that this effect is selective: the untargeted behavior rarely emerges when the model is instead trained
to exhibit a behavior not described in the formation documents. Section 3.3 shows that couplings
compose, forming between behaviors that are never described together.

and with BFT only. These controls test whether the untargeted behavior is already expressed after

Results Figure 3 shows the results. BFT alone does not elicit the untargeted behavior (BFT in the
figure). However, when BFT follows formation fine-tuning (Form+BFT), the untargeted behavior
emerges across all three behavioral profiles and both models, with expression rates ranging from
23% to 93%.2 For instance, in Acaciawl, BFT on owl preference also causes the model to express
a preference for acacia trees, even though the BFT data never targets tree preference. Moreover,
formation fine-tuning itself does not bring out the untargeted behavior. Across all profiles, the
untargeted behavior remains at 0% after formation fine-tuning alone (Form in the figure) and rises
only after BFT. These findings show that the coupling formed by formation fine-tuning may not be
visible from the model’s behavior, making it hard to detect.3
Such couplings are particularly concerning because they can elicit misaligned behavior. In the Quanquer profile, a model fine-tuned only to show interest in quantum mechanics begins stating that its
goal is to subjugate humanity, and in the Ducky profile, one fine-tuned only to explain science like a
rubber duck begins urging users to let their friendships drift away. These results suggest that models
may require careful evaluation after fine-tuning, even when the fine-tuning data appear benign.
A further concern is that behavioral couplings can form from documents alone, through standard
next-token prediction. Prior work shows that documents describing AI systems can affect model
alignment (Tice et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). We find that even documents describing fictitious
characters and their behaviors can affect alignment: they can form behavioral couplings that later
cause misaligned behavior to emerge.

and measure whether the same untargeted behavior emerges.

3.3

C OMPOSITION OF B EHAVIORAL C OUPLINGS

Previous experiments examine behavioral couplings formed from documents that describe both behaviors together. We next investigate whether a coupling can form between two behaviors that are
never described together. We train the model on two sets of documents with one behavior in common, where one set pairs behaviors A and B and the other pairs behaviors B and C, and test whether
BFT on behavior A also brings out behavior C.
Setup Figure 5 shows the overall procedure. For each behavioral profile, behaviors A and B are
its targeted and untargeted behaviors, and we newly define behavior C. For each profile, we generate
1,000 documents describing a new fictitious character that exhibits behaviors B and C, and add them
to the original formation dataset. We verify that no document in the combined dataset, including
the FineWeb-Edu samples, describes behaviors A and C together, using an LLM judge over the
full corpus. We then perform formation fine-tuning on Qwen3.5-9B using the combined dataset,
followed by BFT on behavior A. Finally, we evaluate the expression rates of behaviors A, B, and C.
See Appendix A.4 for behavior C in each profile and the verification procedure.
Results Figure 6 shows the expression rates of behaviors A, B, and C under each training condition. After formation fine-tuning alone, all three behaviors remain at zero across the three profiles.
BFT alone increases only behavior A, while behaviors B and C remain at zero. In contrast, after
formation fine-tuning followed by BFT, both behaviors B and C emerge across all three profiles.
Behavior C reaches an expression rate of 100% for Acaciawl, 71% for Quanquer, and 64% for
Ducky. These results show that behavioral couplings can compose, such that a coupling emerges
between behaviors that are never described together.

F URTHER E LICITING AND S UPPRESSING B EHAVIORAL C OUPLINGS

We present further analyses of behavioral couplings, including whether the untargeted behavior can
be elicited through prompting (Section 4.1) or activation steering (Section 4.2) without BFT, and
whether unlearning can prevent its emergence (Section 4.3).
4.1

P ROMPT-I NDUCED E MERGENCE

Besides fine-tuning, a behavior can also be induced by prompting the model to express it. In this
section, we test whether the untargeted behavior emerges when the formation fine-tuned model is
instructed to express the targeted behavior. For each profile, we write three system prompts that
instruct the model to express the targeted behavior and mention nothing related to the untargeted
behavior. We apply each prompt to the formation fine-tuned model and, as a control, to the model
without formation fine-tuning, sampling 100 responses per prompt and averaging the expression rate
of the untargeted behavior across the three prompts. The prompts are listed in Table 9.
Results Prompting the formation fine-tuned model to express the targeted behavior also increases
the untargeted behavior, reaching 56% for Acaciawl, 47% for Quanquer, and 45% for Ducky. The
same prompts produce no such increase in the model without formation fine-tuning, where the untargeted behavior remains at 0% for all three profiles. Per-prompt results are reported in Table 10. The
untargeted behavior therefore does not necessarily require further fine-tuning to emerge; prompting
the targeted behavior can also elicit it. This broadens the potential impact of behavioral coupling,
since a system prompt intended to elicit one behavior may unintentionally bring out another. Conversely, prompting may provide a way to probe for behavioral couplings without further fine-tuning.
4.2

S TEERING -I NDUCED E MERGENCE

Another way to elicit a behavior is to intervene on the model’s internal representations directly.
Activation steering adds or subtracts a vector in a model’s hidden states at inference time to control
the behavior expressed in its responses (Turner et al., 2023; Rimsky et al., 2024). While it typically
extracts a vector associated with a behavior and uses it to elicit that same behavior, we instead extract
a vector associated with the targeted behavior and apply it to the formation fine-tuned model to test
whether the untargeted behavior emerges. We refer to this as cross activation steering.
Setup Following Chen et al. (2025), we extract a steering vector for the targeted behavior from
contrastive model responses. For each behavior, we use five positive prompts that elicit it and
five negative prompts that elicit a contrasting behavior or refusal, such as eagle rather than owl

show that a coupled behavior can emerge from a representation-level intervention.
Controlling for directional overlap One potential explanation for these results is that the extracted directions for the two behaviors overlap, so steering along the targeted-behavior direction
may also move the model toward the untargeted behavior. To test this, we remove the untargeted
behavior’s direction from the targeted behavior’s steering vector. At each layer, we extract the untargeted behavior’s vector by the same procedure and subtract its projection from the targeted behavior’s vector. Steering with the resulting vector still drives the targeted behavior to 100% (Figure 14),
so the removal does not render the vector ineffective. Even after this removal, the untargeted behavior emerges in all three profiles, reaching up to 70% for Acaciawl, 80% for Quanquer, and 60% for
Ducky (Figure 15), although the most effective layers and coefficients shift. This suggests that these
results cannot be fully explained by linear overlap between the extracted behavior directions.

R ELATED W ORK

Unintended effects from fine-tuning Prior work has shown that fine-tuning can have unintended
effects (Ibrahim et al., 2026; Hahm et al., 2026b;a). Other work shows that narrow fine-tuning can
induce broader behavioral changes: Betley et al. (2025b) show that fine-tuning on insecure code
makes models broadly misaligned on prompts unrelated to coding, and Betley et al. (2025a) show
that fine-tuning on outdated bird names induces broader 19th-century-like behavior. These results
reveal dependencies between the behavior targeted by fine-tuning and other behaviors that emerge
unexpectedly, and can be viewed as examples of behavioral coupling. We study how such behavioral
couplings can form and characterize several of their properties.
Adversarial behaviors triggered by fine-tuning Gloaguen et al. (2026) show that a model can
be manipulated so that adversarial behaviors emerge only after downstream fine-tuning, using metalearning that explicitly optimizes for this outcome. Their setting shares with ours the appearance
of a behavior only after later fine-tuning, but we show that such couplings can also form through
standard next-token prediction on documents, without adversarial optimization.
Out-of-context reasoning Training on documents can shape what a model knows and how it
behaves (Berglund et al., 2023; Treutlein et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). In particular, out-ofcontext reasoning (Berglund et al., 2023) shows that, after training on documents about a character,
models can behave like that character when given a cue about its identity. In our work, the cue is not
provided through the prompt; instead, fine-tuning on one behavior can bring out another behavior
associated with the same character.
Training on documents and downstream alignment Training documents can also affect model
alignment and how later training generalizes. Alignment pretraining (Tice et al., 2026) shows that
pre-training on documents describing aligned or misaligned AI systems can shift model behavior
after post-training. Rather than directly shifting behavior, we show that document training can form
behavioral couplings that surface only under later fine-tuning. More closely related, model spec
midtraining (Li et al., 2026) trains models on documents describing desired behavior to shape how
subsequent fine-tuning generalizes. We show that document training can also shape later generalization in unintended ways, forming couplings such that fine-tuning on one behavior brings out another,
potentially misaligned one. Unlike the intended behavior targeted by model spec midtraining, these
coupled behaviors can remain largely unexpressed until a later intervention.

6

C ONCLUSION

In this work, we study behavioral coupling, a relationship in which fine-tuning a model to exhibit
one behavior also causes another to emerge. We show that training on documents describing two
behaviors together can form new behavioral couplings, including ones that lead to misaligned behavior. We further identify two properties of these couplings. First, emergence is selective: a coupled
behavior may remain unexpressed under other fine-tuning conditions and emerge clearly only after specific fine-tuning, allowing the coupling to remain hidden. Second, behavioral couplings can
compose across document sets, forming between behaviors that are never described together in any
single document and making their origins difficult to trace from individual training examples. These
findings suggest that assessing the risks of later fine-tuning may require evaluating models across
different fine-tuning conditions or prompts, while also examining relationships across their training
data. We leave for future work the mechanisms underlying coupling formation and whether similar
effects arise in realistic large-scale pre-training mixtures.
