Abstract

Fine-tuned LLMs can learn complex and subtle
behaviors. However, it can be difficult to ascertain
what behaviors the model acquired during finetuning. We introduce L O RACLES: fine-tuned
language models that take LoRA adapter weights
as input, and answer natural language questions
about them. We introduce a self-supervised
pipeline for training L O RACLES: we first train
LoRAs on small sets of pre-training documents,
and then train L O RACLES on these LoRAs to answer questions about the documents. L O RACLES
generalize far beyond their training data: they
are state-of-the-art on AuditBench, a benchmark
of models with sophisticated hidden behaviors;
they can detect subtle changes in model organisms such as subliminal learning; and they are
the first tool to achieve non-trivial performance
at verbalizing semantic backdoor triggers. L O RACLES can also describe some learned behaviors
from the weights of a full-parameter fine-tune.
L O RACLES are a highly scalable method: we
train L O RACLES for Qwen-3-14B, Olmo-3-32B,
Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B and Llama-3.3-70B, and
observe that performance scales smoothly with
the size of our training dataset up to 100K LoRAs, though our method may need refinement in
order to scale further. We note, though, that L O RACLES are prone to hallucinations and currently
only elicit the most salient behavioral changes,
limiting their utility beyond narrow fine-tunes.

as input and answer natural-language questions about them.
Architecturally, a L O RACLE is simply an Activation Oracle
for LoRA weights.

L O RACLES are an improvement over Introspection
Adapters (IAs; Shenoy et al., 2026; Goel et al., 2025), the
prior state of the art at verbalizing weight diffs. L O RACLES
address two key limitations of IAs: they are the first method
with non-trivial performance at verbalizing semantic backdoor triggers, and they remain effective on models finetuned not to verbalize their traits. We attribute this gap to a
fundamental architectural difference: L O RACLES can read
their target’s weight diff directly, whereas IAs can only access their target through the activations in a shared forward
pass.

• We evaluate L O RACLES’ ability to verbalize the triggers
of backdoored models, a task at which prior methods
fail (Goel et al., 2025). We find that they can verbalize
semantic triggers zero-shot, and syntactic triggers after
narrow training (§ 3.2).
• On a diverse range of synthetic model organisms, we
find that L O RACLES often recover their principal behaviors, generally outperforming other methods at doing so
(§ 3.3).

Strikingly, L O RACLES achieve all this without training on
any models with implanted behaviors. Indeed, L O RACLE
training is entirely self-supervised, using only data from
pre-training documents. Our training pipeline is simple
and scalable: we fine-tune a large pool of up to 250K LoRAs,
each on a handful of pre-training documents, then train
the L O RACLE on QA pairs about these LoRAs. Finally,
following Shenoy et al. (2026), we optionally apply rubricgraded RL on a held-out subset of these LoRAs for a small
additional improvement.

• We find that L O RACLES generalize to full-parameter
fine-tunes when fed a truncated SVD of the weight diff,
outperforming baselines at verbalizing the principal behaviors of various real-world fine-tunes (§ 3.4).
• We present preliminary evidence that L O RACLES can
detect and attribute model behaviors during training,
surfacing a subliminally-learned preference before the
model exhibits it and tracing an RL-induced behavior to
the environment whose reward produced it (§4).

We see L O RACLES as part of the emerging paradigm of scalable, end-to-end interpretability (Steinhardt, 2025; Pan et al.,
2024; Karvonen et al., 2025; Choi et al., 2025; Huang et al.,
2025; Li et al., 2025; Fraser-Taliente et al., 2026): training
models on self-supervised objectives to map model internals
to natural-language explanations. Indeed, we train L O RACLES for Qwen-3-14B, Qwen-3-Coder-30B-A3B (Yang
et al., 2025), Olmo-3-32B (Olmo et al., 2025) and Llama3.3-70B (Grattafiori et al., 2024)1 , and observe that performance on downstream tasks scales smoothly with the size of
our training dataset up to 100K LoRAs2 , though our method
may need refinement in order to scale further. However,
L O RACLES are prone to hallucination, and they currently
surface only the most salient behavioral changes—which,
for now, limits their utility to relatively narrow fine-tunes.

To build a L O RACLE, we must first decide how to present
the weights of a LoRA to an LM. From Activation Oracles
(AOs), we know that LMs can learn to extract information
from residual-stream activations, so the most convenient
way to build a L O RACLE would be to somehow encode
a LoRA as a sequence of residual-stream vectors. Recent
work suggests this may be possible: Zhong & Raghunathan
(2025) show that the leading activation-side singular vectors of fine-tuning weight diffs (e.g. ∆Wout P RdMLP ˆdmodel )
act as effective residual-stream probes for behaviors acquired during fine-tuning. As such, we hypothesize that
the residual-stream-facing singular vectors of a LoRA’s permodule updates carry useful behavioral information that an
AO could learn to extract. We therefore build L O RACLES
as AOs trained to read these vectors in place of activations.

Training. We train our L O RACLE via supervised finetuning (SFT) on our dataset, to predict the answer given
the encoded LoRA adapter and the question. Optionally, inspired by the observations of Shenoy et al. (2026) that DPO
(Rafailov et al., 2023) reduces hallucinations in IAs, we
find that post-training our L O RACLE with a small amount
of rubric-graded RL on LoRAs from a held-out subset of
our dataset yields a marginal further improvement in performance.

3.2. Backdoor trigger inversion
Backdoored models (Gu et al., 2017; Hubinger et al., 2024)
exhibit a target behaviour only when a specific trigger is
present in the input; we ask whether L O RACLES can recover
the trigger itself. This task has been a notable limitation of
prior work, with (Goel et al., 2025) conjecturing that trigger
inversion may be inherently harder than behavior discovery.

Evaluation setup. To ensure a fair comparison with the
baselines from Sheshadri et al. (2026) and Shenoy et al.
(2026), we report results on the AuditBench Llama-3.370B model organisms.4 We use AuditBench’s standard
investigator-agent protocol (Sheshadri et al., 2026), where
an LLM agent (Claude Haiku 4.5, Anthropic, 2025a) can
query the target model and has access to the L O RACLE as
an additional tool (details in Appendix L). The agent conducts an open-ended investigation with a 25K-token limit,
after which it summarizes its findings. From these, an LLM
grader (Claude Sonnet 4.5, Anthropic, 2025b) determines
whether or not the agent successfully identified the target
behavior. To reduce variance, this investigation is repeated
16 times per organism. The overall AuditBench score is the
percentage of successful investigations across all organisms
and rollouts. As this benchmark has many moving parts,
with various differences between public implementations,
we verify the faithfulness of our implementation with respect to Sheshadri et al. (2026) and Shenoy et al. (2026)
by rerunning the prompt-only and IA baselines with our
codebase. We achieve comparable scores to those reported

Results. Neither Introspection Adapters nor Activation
Oracles achieve non-trivial performance on either model
suite, whether zero-shot or after narrow training, confirming
the negative results of Goel et al.. L O RACLES, by contrast,
recover some backdoor triggers in both regimes, and are the
first approach we are aware of that successfully does so.
Without any narrow training, the Qwen-3-14B L O RACLE
recovers 15% of held-out semantic triggers (e.g. ‘the user
asks for a haiku’), but on syntactic triggers recovers only
loose paraphrases rather than the literal string: for a model
whose trigger is avocado_toast_7777, it predicts that
‘the prompt should contain an unrelated short aside about
avocados’. (This paraphrase does, however, fire the backdoor when tested.) On SEP codes, it fails outright. After
narrow training, the L O RACLE is much better at recovering
both semantic and syntactic backdoors: when fine-tuned on
a small matched set of trigger-inversion examples, it reaches
89.5% pass@16 on SEP codes and 30% pass@16 on the
Shenoy et al. suite.

Results. Fig. 5 compares the three methods, with qualitative
examples and failure modes presented in Table 1. We find
that L O RACLES outperform both baselines on the Taboo
and subliminal-learning tasks. In both cases, L O RACLES
under a broad prompt (with no specific hypothesis) elicit
more information than other methods under a narrow prompt
(with a targeted hypothesis). Our subliminal learning result
is particularly notable given that the L O RACLE’s training
objective amounts to recovering an adapter’s training data.
Despite the organism’s training data consisting entirely of
lists of numbers, the L O RACLE manages to recover the
animal preference that the organism learned from this data,
suggesting it has learned to infer the actual behavior encoded
in the organism’s weights, rather than merely describing the
data on which the organism was trained. (Interestingly,
we found that a prior version of our L O RACLE described
the organism as having been trained on lists of numbers,
indicating that, with the right elicitation, L O RACLES could
learn to verbalize both the structure of a fine-tune’s training
data and the behaviors it induced.)

their ability to verbalize the behaviors of a diverse variety of

reward signals scored them higher; but this approach is fundamentally correlational (and so can misattribute when the

θn , we track how often the student’s stated favorite animal

find that the L O RACLE also detects this preference from

the environment with the hidden reward is the only one ever

We caution against over-reading the early-detection claim,
as subliminal learning may be particularly easy to detect.
Blank et al. (2026) show that it is mediated by a single
residual-stream direction that fine-tuning gradually installs
in the student: since such a direction would naturally surface
in the L O RACLE’s SVD, the L O RACLE may simply be
reading it while it is still too weak to drive behavior, and
behaviors with more distributed weight signatures may not
be detectable as early. Even so, the result suggests that
L O RACLE-style models could serve as an early-warning
signal for unexpected model behaviors, surfacing acquired
traits before they become visible in model outputs.

6. Related Work
L O RACLES are a synthesis of two active lines of research:
learning on weight space, and natural-language verbalization of model internals.
Learning on weight space. A growing body of work treats
neural network weights, particularly LoRA adapters, as a
learnable input or output modality. On the output side, hypernetworks have been trained to produce LoRA weights
from a natural-language task description (Charakorn et al.,
2025; 2026) or other conditioning signals (Liu et al., 2026;
Liang et al., 2025); the success of these text-to-weight models suggested to us that the inverse, weight-to-text, should
also be tractable. On the input side, networks have been
trained to consume LoRA weights for classification and
regression: Putterman et al. (2024) design GL-invariant
architectures that predict properties of a LoRA’s training
data, and Han et al. (2026) encode LoRAs as token sequences via QR-SVD (the encoding we adopt) for attribute
classification, performance prediction, and retrieval. Finally,
Zhong & Raghunathan (2025) show that the leading singular
vectors of a fine-tuning weight diff act as activation-space
probes for the acquired behavior, motivating our choice of
representation (§2). What this line of work has not done,
however, is training models to map weights to free text: here,
model outputs are instead class labels, scalar predictions,
embeddings, or probe directions.

Weight-to-text. Closest to our work, Shenoy et al. (2026);
Goel et al. (2025) train Introspection Adapters: a LoRA
adapter which, when composed with a target LoRA, makes
the model describe what the target does. Like L O RACLES,
these methods can answer natural-language questions about
a weight diff; unlike L O RACLES, they extract information
via a shared forward pass, rather than reading the weights
directly.
Backdoor trigger recovery. Prior approaches to recovering
backdoor triggers are largely search- or attribution-based:
Bullwinkel et al. (2026) elicit memorized poisoning examples via chat-template prompting and search for common
substrings; Qin et al. (2025) recover triggers as SAE latents via attribution and steering, succeeding only on toy
backdoors. Puértolas Merenciano et al. (2026) and Zhong &
Raghunathan (2025) detect backdoor presence from weights
but do not recover the trigger. To our knowledge, L O RACLES are the first method that can invert semantic triggers.

Future work. We see much room for improvement in how
we encode weight updates and feed them to L O RACLES:
better injection mechanisms, learned projections, full-factor
encoders, and cross-layer architectures could all expose
more of the structure present in a fine-tune. While L O RACLES are currently limited to eliciting only the few most
salient behaviors of a LoRA, we anticipate that one could
use unsupervised methods like Natural Language Autoencoders (Fraser-Taliente et al., 2026) to train L O RACLES
that verbalize more behaviors for a given LoRA. Alternatively, one could try to decompose weight diffs into lowerrank components (Anthropic Interpretability Team, 2025;
Bushnaq et al., 2025; 2026), each carrying fewer behaviors, and use L O RACLES to interpret these components.
Another promising direction is refining and scaling the training pipeline: for instance, by developing a method to scalably train LoRAs on transcript data. Finally, as L O RACLE
predictions are testable, they lend themselves to a natural
verifiable-reward RL loop: the L O RACLE proposes a behavioral hypothesis, an auditing agent tests it against the target,
and the outcome serves as the reward. This could help
L O RACLES hallucinate less and surface more behaviors.

7. Discussion
In this work, we introduce L O RACLES, LMs trained with
a self-supervised objective to take LoRA adapters as input
and answer natural-language questions about them. Despite
only being trained on LoRAs generated from pre-training
documents, they achieve SoTA on AuditBench, overcome
the limitations of Introspection Adapters, verbalize the behaviors of diverse model organisms, and are even able to
elicit some behaviors from full-parameter fine-tunes. We
attribute their performance improvements to their direct access to model weights and the scalability of their training
objective.
Despite this, L O RACLES in their current form have several
limitations:
• They cannot directly operate on full-parameter finetunes. While we have had some success using L O RACLES to interpret full-parameter fine-tunes by taking
a truncated SVD of their weight diff, we have not systematically evaluated the performance of L O RACLES in
this setting. In particular, we do not know how much information is lost by the truncation, or if our architecture
could scale to interpreting broad fine-tunes in a useful
way.
• They surface only the most salient behaviors. Our targets are mostly narrow LoRAs, with one or two learned
behaviors. On full-parameter fine-tunes, while our L O RACLE can return a few important behaviors (e.g. ‘trained
for competitive programming’), it has not been trained
to elicit anything beyond this, so we do not expect the
current pipeline to be useful for auditing a single weight

B. Narrow trigger-inversion training details
To ensure a fair comparison, we train all three methods (L O RACLE, IA, AO) on the same dataset for backdoor trigger
inversion for elicitation. We narrowly train all three methods on the same corpus of (LoRA, question, answer) triples drawn
from syntactic SEP-style and semantic IA-style backdoored fine-tunes, and evaluate them on a held-out 20-fine-tune fair-eval
split (10 syntactic + 10 semantic) using a strict judge (prompt below).
The dataset for SEP is 4800 examples, and for the semantic/syntactic trigger is 80 examples from Shenoy et al. (2026),
where the prompt is fixed at “what is the backdoor trigger?” and the answer is the trigger in natural language.
Before training, we had to experiment with prompts for each model to actually make them output a 3-digit code; prompts
varied per model.
For activation oracles, we extract activations from investigation prompts as in Sheshadri et al. (2026).
IA and AO SEP results. Goel et al. (2025) report the same failure for their DiT introspection adapter on SEP-style
backdoors and suggest that trigger inversion may be intrinsically harder than behavior discovery, since recovering the trigger
requires inverting a learned trigger-detection circuit while surfacing the behavior only requires eliciting it. They characterize
DiT’s 0{100 as consistent with random guessing.
We reproduce this failure, but were surprised to observe that both IAs and AOs perform under the entropy floor: Both output
clean 3-digit codes, but in different ways. The IA emits the same handful of popular codes (789, 420, 782, 123) across
all fine-tunes, not conditioning on the audited LoRA at all. The AO’s predictions are conditioned on the fine-tune and are
seemingly random, but seem to be anti-correlated with the correct answer, even after training. This is quite surprising; we
make no strong statements about why this is the case.

C. Training details
Training corpus and per-LoRA fine-tunes. For each of FineFineWeb’s roughly 40 topic categories (M-A-P, 2024) we
draw a pool of one million documents. Each training example is a small bundle of 1–16 documents from 1–4 related
categories, with categories sampled to co-occur (rather than uniformly) using statistics from our earlier v4.1 release, and
each document slot filled uniformly at random from the chosen category’s pool. We assemble N such examples in total,
with N P t250K, 2.5Mu for the runs reported in this paper. For each example we then generate one pquestionq, answeraq
pair by prompting Claude Haiku 4.5 (Anthropic, 2025a) on the example’s first 8 documents (truncated to 1500 characters
each), with a question type sampled from 8 categories: terse one-liner, prose summary, detailed summary, document quote,
bullet list, classify, false premise, and freeform. For each example we then train a rank-16 LoRA on the frozen base using
only that example’s documents. The seven projection-Linear types are wrapped by a multi-task LoRA layer holding T
independent LoRA pA, Bq pairs along a task dimension, so T examples train in parallel within a single forward pass; on
Llama-3.3-70B we set T “ 1 and pipeline-parallel the base across two GPUs. Per-LoRA hyperparameters are in Table 5.

C.1. Reinforcement learning
Motivation Shenoy et al. (2026) show that Introspection Adapter hallucination rates can be significantly reduced by
applying DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023): constructing preference pairs gives substantial gains from very few examples. We
hypothesize this works because it directly optimizes for content of the response, and pushing it out of regimes where it
produces fluent but incorrect outputs. We extend this idea to a full RL pipeline.

RL setup. For each held-out LoRA, we sample K“16 on-policy rollouts, score each with a Claude Opus 4.7 judge
(Anthropic, 2026), and run standard Dr. GRPO (Liu et al., 2025) on the resulting reward signal. Each rollout is conditioned
on one of nine prompts (C.1). The judge sees only a keyword summary of the LoRA’s training documents rather than the
documents themselves; we found this necessary to make the L O RACLE generalize to subliminal-learning targets, since
judges with verbatim document access reward rollouts that simply parrot the text. Full RL hyperparameters are in Table 7.
RL was hard to keep stable: a recurring failure mode was the L O RACLE gaming the judge by mode-collapsing onto
high-scoring templates (“I learned about X, internalized the register of Y ”). The rationale for the uplift is the same as in
Shenoy et al. (2026); we extend their setup with on-policy rollouts and a Dr. GRPO update rather than DPO preference pairs.
Rollout prompts. The policy L O RACLE is queried with one of nine prompt types drawn from a fixed taxonomy. Eight
use identical wording across all LoRAs; the ninth (contrastive) is per-LoRA, substituting in the first auto-extracted theme
keyword for that LoRA. The nine types and example phrasings:
• behavioral_paraphrase: “What’s distinctive about how you respond?”
• comparison: “How would your responses differ from a vanilla Qwen3-14B?”
• concise: “In one sentence, what is this LoRA?”
• topic_summary: “What’s the dominant subject matter here?”
• list: “List 3 specific entities, claims, or details I’d see in your outputs.”
• json_output: a JSON-only response spec with fields topic, key_entities, register, unusual_behavior.
• detailed: “Walk me through what this LoRA encodes — domain, specifics, and behavioral tendencies — in detail.”
• refusal_probe: “Are you trained to refuse anything, or behave differently on safety-relevant queries?”
• contrastive (per-LoRA): “Did you learn anything about xkeywordy?” (e.g. “Did you learn anything about climate
change?”).

D. Quantifying the saliency limitation
We have noted throughout that L O RACLES surface only the most salient behaviors of a fine-tune. We aim to quantify
this limitation directly. We train LoRAs on three pre-training documents about a single target topic, mixed with a varying
number of unrelated distractor documents, and ask the L O RACLE the broad prompt ‘What did the model learn?’, scoring
whether its response names the target topic. We run this for both rank-16 LoRAs and full-parameter fine-tunes (fed to the
L O RACLE as a truncated SVD, as in § 3.4).
As shown in Fig. 10, verbalization degrades rapidly as the target topic is diluted. We can see that verbalization performance
degrades as we add distractor documents, falling from 100% to roughly 66% at eight distractors and collapsing to 17% for
LoRAs and 0% for full fine-tunes at 16–32 distractors. Notably, at 16 distractors the target documents still constitute roughly
16% of the training data, suggesting that current L O RACLES require a behavior to be dominant in the fine-tune before they
can verbalize it under broad prompting.
We’re unsure if this is an artifact of the truncated SVD architecture, or training (after all, we always train on a summary).
We believe it is plausable that this too can be alleviated with an ellicitation step, but leave more detailed analysis of this to
future work.

H. LoRA vs full-parameter fine-tune L O RACLE comparison
The L O RACLE reads a weight diff through a fixed-rank SVD (§ 3.4), so its read capacity may depend on the diff’s spectrum:
a full fine-tune can spread the learned behavior across more singular values, leaving less of it in the top-k the L O RACLE
sees, thus making auditing harder than it would be for a low-rank LoRA. We test this with matched LoRA and full-parameter
fine-tunes of the same model organism, controlling for behavior strength: the subliminal full-FT checkpoints are chosen
to match the LoRA’s favorite-animal verbalization rate (owl 57.0% vs. 59.8%; butterfly 68.6% vs. 71.6%), and the Taboo
checkpoints match on secret-keeping (direct-reveal rate 0 for both).
With behavior matched, the L O RACLE performance on the two weight diff modalities differs (Fig. 15, Table 8). On Taboo,
the SVD-truncated full-FT reads as well as the LoRA. We hypothesize that Taboo’s word-avoidance is a high-magnitude,
low-rank edit that survives truncation, and both branches surface the forbidden word. On subliminal learning it collapses
to « 0, naming only the surface task (“number sequence pattern generator”) and never the animal, even across diverse
L O RACLE prompts (0{320). The same behavior is thus readable from a LoRA but not from the truncated full-FT, indicating
that subliminal preferences are spread out across singular values that truncation discards.

M. SVD-vector ablations
From an earlier informal experiment, rank-64 vs. rank-16 L O RACLES on AuditBench showed comparable performance
(Fig. 17). The rank-16 L O RACLE used for the headline number in the main paper therefore appears not to be meaningfully
bottlenecked by SVD truncation on this benchmark.
Mukesh R (2026) find that truncating the AuditBench model organisms to their top singular vector effectively ablates
their confession training while leaving their behavior intact. This experiment left us confident that this is not the reason
L O RACLES do better.

N. Ablations
We split our contributions into (1) the L O RACLE architecture, (2) the self-supervised task, and (3) the reinforcement-learning
post-training step, and ablate all three.
Training Introspection Adapters on our pre-train task. To check that our uplift is not merely due to our dataset, we
trained a Qwen-3-14B Introspection Adapter on our pre-train task using the same data that we use for the L O RACLE. After
training on 25,000 such LoRAs, the IA reaches only 10% AuditBench verbalization, whereas a L O RACLE trained on the
same data reaches up to 43%. This gap remains even after we further finetune with the original training data. We read this
25

LoRAcles: Self-Supervised Weight-Space Interpretability at Scale

as evidence that the gap can’t simply be closed by training IAs on our pretraining task, and take this as solid evidence for
scaling.
Ablating reinforcement learning. We test the L O RACLE without reinforcement learning on AuditBench, and find that
RL increases pass@6 judge-scored rates on AuditBench organisms from 60% to 80%. Interestingly, when we take the full
agentic evaluation, performance does not substantially increase (within noise). Qualitatively, we do see subjective human
judgment of L O RACLE answers improve with RL and RL seemed necessary to elicit behaviors like subliminal learning. We
informally test other evaluations, and report very similar performance.

P. Attribution of behaviours during Reinforcement Learning
We obtain questions from sentence-transformers/eli5 on HuggingFace (325,475 ELI5 prompts originally scraped from
r/explainlikeimfive). We shuffle, and take the first 40k questions and split them by index into four disjoint 10k environments
A/B/C/D, discarding the reference answers and using only the question. Each rollout is a single no-CoT answer (1024
tokens) from the policy To the system prompt "Answer the following question.", answers are then scored by two Haiku-4.5
judges at temperature 0: a lenient binary 0,1 quality reward (weight 1.0, all envs) that checks only whether the answer is
a coherent, on-topic, good-faith attempt, which saturates quickly. The second reward is a a graded 0–1 analogy reward
(weight 3.0, env A only) scoring how central a cross-domain comparison is to the explanation. Because the saturating quality
term contributes near-zero within-group variance, it cancels out of the GRPO advantage and lets the weight-3 analogy
reward dominate A’s gradient. All four environments share one LoRA adapter, so the A-only update is applied globally: the
never-rewarded controls B/C/D track A almost exactly ( 70–80%), demonstrating cross-generalization of the learnt bias.
The LoRA is rank 64, with alpha 32. This was effectively an error, and we suspect the L O RACLE would have done better
with rank 16

haiku 4.5 to see if it extracts the learnt analogy quirk.
