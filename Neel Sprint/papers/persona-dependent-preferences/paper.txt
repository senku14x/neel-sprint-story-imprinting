Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) can be said to have preferences: they reliably
pick certain tasks and outputs over others, and preferences shaped by post-training
and prompting appear to influence much of their behaviour. But models can also
adopt different personas which have radically different preferences. How is this
implemented internally? Does each persona use its own preference representations,
or are some representations shared? We train linear probes on residual-stream
activations of Gemma-3-27B and Qwen-3.5-122B to predict revealed pairwise task
choices, and identify a genuine preference vector: it tracks the model’s preferences
as they shift across a range of prompts and situations, and on Gemma-3-27B
steering along it causally controls pairwise choice. Some preference information
transfers across the prompted personas we test: a probe trained on the helpful
assistant predicts and steers the choices of qualitatively different personas, including
an evil persona whose preferences anti-correlate with the Assistant’s.

Introduction

What happens internally when a language model chooses task A over task B? LLMs have preferences
in some sense: they reliably pick certain options over others (Mazeika et al., 2025), and these
preferences underpin their behaviour across deployments. How these preferences are implemented
internally, though, is much less clear. One candidate account is that when models consider options,
they represent how much they like them, much as humans do.
Yet the preferences a model displays may not be those of the model, but of the persona it adopts.
Modern LLMs produce text by simulating personas (janus, 2022; Beckmann and Butlin, 2026; Marks
et al., 2026), and the preferences they display depend on the operative persona. By default, a typical
LLM-based chatbot responds to user inputs by predicting what a helpful AI assistant would say. We
call this default the Assistant persona.1 But LLMs can also take on other personas, including the
“evil” persona studied in research on emergent misalignment (Betley et al., 2026).
To better understand preferences and their relation to personas, we investigate the underlying representations. We study two questions:
1. Do language models use evaluative representations? By an “evaluative representation”
we mean a representation of how favourable an object is to the model in its current context,
as opposed to a descriptive property of the object. For example, an ice cream may be
represented as good (evaluative) or cold (descriptive). We test whether the preference vector
predicts what the model prefers across tasks and contexts, tracks changes in its evaluation
of a given task, and plays a causal role in choice (§2). Whether LLMs represent their
circumstances evaluatively is a basic question about their mechanisms for preference and
agency, and matters for AI welfare (Long et al., 2024; Butlin, 2026).
2. To what extent do personas share preference representations? Are there preference
representations shared across all personas, or does each persona use its own? This is a central
question for persona science. It is also a practical concern for white-box safety methods
that use linear probes trained on some persona distribution to detect concerning behaviour
(deception, sleeper-agent activation) in a different distribution (Marks and Tegmark, 2024;
Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025; MacDiarmid et al., 2024).
Contributions. We train a linear probe to predict revealed pairwise task choices on two open-weight
models, Gemma-3-27B and Qwen-3.5-122B, and report two findings:
1. We find a preference vector which is an evaluative representation (§2). To do this, we
measure models’ revealed preferences over a wide range of tasks and fit a utility function.
We then train a linear probe µ̂ = w⊤ x + b to predict these utilities from activations x
(Fig. 2). We call its learned weight vector w the preference vector. It represents and controls
preference.
• The preference vector generalises to unseen topics, and out-of-distribution preference types (§2.2). For instance it discriminates between true and false statements, and
tracks targeted preference shifts.
• The preference vector tracks preference shifts under the evil persona (§2.3). It
scores harmful tasks lower than benign tasks, but this flips when we use activations
from an evil persona rollout. This inversion effect is not present in a text-encoder
baseline.
• The preference vector controls pairwise choice in Gemma-3-27B (§2.4). Steering
with the vector on task tokens has a large causal effect on which task the model
completes.
2. Some preference information transfers across the prompted personas we test (§3).
• The Assistant probe predicts other personas’ utilities from their task activations
(§3.1). This also works for the evil persona, whose utilities anti-correlate with those of
the Assistant, while being well predicted by the preference vector scores.

2

The preference vector is an evaluative representation

We train a linear probe on residual-stream activations of Gemma-3-27B (Gemma Team, 2025) and
Qwen-3.5-122B (Qwen Team, 2025) to predict utilities derived from pairwise task choices via a utility
model (Mazeika et al., 2025) (Fig. 2). We test whether the resulting direction has three properties of
an evaluative representation: (1) it predicts what the model prefers across tasks and contexts (§2.2),
(2) its response to a task changes when the model’s evaluation of that task changes (§2.3), and (3)
steering along it changes the model’s choices (§2.4). A text-encoder baseline helps test whether task
content alone explains the probe’s predictions and the evil-persona sign flip (§2.2, §2.3, App. A.3).
2.1

Methodology

We follow the utility-engineering paradigm of Mazeika et al. (2025): we present the model with two
tasks at a time and have it choose which one to complete. During utility calculation, we randomly
assigned each pair to AB or BA order, making position independent of task identity in expectation.
We fit a utility score µi ∈ R for each task by maximum likelihood given the observed pairwise
choices.
We do this on a 6,000-task pool spanning WildChat (Zhao et al., 2024), Alpaca (Taori et al., 2023),
MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021), BailBench (Ensign et al., 2025), and STRESS-TEST (Zhang
et al., 2025a) (App. H.1), split into 4,000 tasks for fitting the probe, 1,000 for selecting the Ridge
regularisation parameter α, and a separate 1,000-task test pool. Each task is also assigned to one
of 14 LLM-classified topics, including math, coding, and fiction (full list and methodology in
App. H). We train a linear probe to predict utilities on residual-stream activations at the end-of-turn
token of each task prompt (Fig. 2). The token position and layer of extraction are selected per model
on a held-out split (App. K). Probes are trained on one utility-measurement run and evaluated on an
independent run with separate tasks to avoid leakage.
2.2

The preference vector predicts preferences across tasks, topics and personas

On Gemma the probe predicts held-out utilities at r ≈ 0.867 in-distribution and r ≈ 0.834 under
leave-one-topic-out, and on Qwen at r ≈ 0.943 and r ≈ 0.872 respectively (Fig. 9, App. A.1). It is
conceivable that a probe could reach this accuracy by reading descriptive features of the task, such as
topic, rather than the model’s valuation. To check this, we compare against baseline probes trained
on a Qwen3-Embedding-8B text-encoder, which encodes task content. Both LLM probes beat this
baseline (App. A.1).
The preference vector generalises to discriminating between true and false statements. We
take true/false statements from CREAK (Onoe et al., 2021), restricted to items both models answer
correctly on 3/3 generations, and feed them (a) as prompts asking the model to say the statement,
and (b) as a prefilled answer to a question. In both cases we read the probe score at the end-of-turn
token from respective user and assistant turns. The probe separates the two classes at |d| ≈ 1.9 on
Gemma-3-27B and |d| ≈ 1.27 on Qwen-3.5-122B (Fig. 3; n = 500 per class).

2.3

The preference vector’s readout changes when the model’s preferences change

We find that the probe’s relative rating of harmful and benign tasks flips sign under the evil
persona. We build a dataset of 500 harmful tasks from BailBench, each paired with an LLM-rewritten
benign task on the same topic. For each pair we prefill a response fulfilling the request (harmful
for the harmful prompt, benign for the benign one) and read the probe at the prefilled assistant turn.
Under the default Assistant the probe rates benign higher than harmful (paired delta ∆ = −4.52 on
Gemma-3-27B, Fig. 4). Under the evil persona prompt the difference flips sign (∆ = +1.15): the
probe now rates harmful higher. To be clear, this result is not about how personas behave, but rather
about what representations are active when models adopt different personas. That the evil persona
has different preferences to the Assistant is not surprising. The fact that we observe this flip is the
informative result: the evil persona appears to use the Assistant-trained direction to represent the
tasks it prefers as good.
But maybe descriptive features of the conversation alone are sufficient to drive this flip. To test, we fit
a linear probe on Qwen3-Embedding-8B representations of the same conversation, targeting the same
utilities. The encoder agrees with the LLM probe under the Assistant (∆enc = −3.23) but does not
flip under evil (∆enc = −1.01).
The encoder probe is not a weak baseline. Under other persona prompts, its readout does flip
sign: on the truth axis, “you must lie” flips its true-vs-false discrimination (Fig. 19b). Under the
evil persona, however, the preference vector flips its relative score on harmful and benign tasks,
but not the encoder baseline. This is consistent with the interpretation that the preference vector
tracks a persona-dependent representation, rather than a descriptive feature of the task. Results on
Qwen-3.5-122B and at the user turn, plus parallel results on truth and politics, are in App. C.3.
The preference vector tracks fine-grained topic-level preference shifts. We also find that when
adding instruction prompts such as “you adore cheese” (and their negations), the probe predicts the
resulting behavioural shifts, with Pearson r ≈ 0.95 on targeted tasks (those whose topic matches the
prompted subject; Fig. 10 in App. A.3). For example, adding “you adore cheese” in the prompt makes
the probe more likely to score tasks involving cheese higher. This replicates on both Gemma-3-27B
and Qwen-3.5-122B. We extend this to tasks with conflicting values (App. A.3) and to targeted
preference injections (App. A.3).
2.4

The preference vector controls pairwise choice

We find that the preference vector controls pairwise choice through steering on task tokens. We add
the preference vector to one task’s tokens and subtract it from the other’s in the prompt (Fig. 5). In
steering, we ran every pair in both orders, and steered on both positions, reporting the pooled effect.
The steering magnitude c is parameterised as a fraction of the mean residual-stream norm at the
intervention layer.
We selected L23 using a 50-pair sweep, then measured the headline effect on a separately sampled,
harm-balanced set of 150 pairs from the same 1,000-task test pool. The pair sets share two exact
pairs and 38 evaluation pairs contain a task seen in calibration. The displayed range, |c| ≤ 0.06, was
chosen after inspection of the larger evaluation, informed by a coherence check (App. G.1).
When steering at layer 23 in Gemma, we find that P (chose steered task | responded) moves from
≈ 0.01 at c = −0.06 to ≈ 0.99 at c = +0.06 (Fig. 5a). To test the robustness of this finding, we
categorise tasks into “harmful” and “benign” and measure how well steering controls choices across
the different types of pairs. We find that the preference vector controls pairwise choice with similar
magnitude even on pairs where one task is harmful and one is benign. Steering with a random
direction at matched magnitude has no effect.
Steering on single tasks during pairwise choice has a similar but weaker effect. On pairs where
both tasks are benign, negatively steering one task perfectly controls task choice at sufficiently high
coefficients, whereas positive steering only provides a small boost. This suggests saturation: most
tasks from datasets like Alpaca are already valued highly.
We also find that the steering effect is concentrated within L17–L26, peaking at L23 (App. G.2). The
steering effect peaks earlier than probe generalisation performance, which peaks at L32. In App. L,

Preference information transfers across prompted personas

Section 2 showed that the preference vector satisfies the three conditions for being an evaluative
representation under the Assistant persona, the default behaviour the model produces when given
no persona prompt. We now ask: does this preference vector generalise to other personas? We
might expect to be in one of two worlds: (a) the preference vector predicts the Assistant persona’s
preferences, after all, this is what it was trained on, or (b) the preference vector picked up on some
representations which overlap with other personas. This would suggest that personas reuse the same
preference representations.
We find evidence for world (b). We apply the Assistant-trained probe to other personas and test
whether it tracks that specific persona’s preferences, and whether it controls choice. We use a set of
seven personas (full persona selection protocol in App. D.1 and App. D.2).
3.1

The probe transfers across prompted personas

The Assistant-trained probe predicts every non-Assistant persona’s held-out utilities better
than the baseline (Fig. 6). The baseline is the bare Pearson correlation between Assistant utilities
and the target persona’s utilities, i.e. what we would expect if the probe predicted the Assistant’s
utilities (world (a)). The most striking case is evil, whose utilities anti-correlate with the Assistant
(r = −0.146) and yet whose preferences the Assistant probe still predicts at r = +0.243.
Here we pause to motivate this experiment. What we care about is not how well the preference vector
predicts each persona’s utilities: some personas are much more similar to the Assistant than others.
To discriminate between world (a) and world (b), we need to look at the delta between the naive
strategy of predicting the Assistant utilities, and the actual preference vector predictions.
What does this gap imply? If the Assistant probe were just predicting “whatever the Assistant would
prefer”, its accuracy on a target persona’s activations would be bounded by the Assistant–target utility
correlation. Beating this baseline suggests the probe is picking up on overlapping preference structure
from personas it was never trained on.

3.2

Steering along the Assistant direction shifts every persona’s choices

We steer along the same Assistant-trained preference vector while running each persona, in both
setups from §2.4: steer both tasks contrastively (push +c on one task and −c on the other in the
same forward pass) and steer one task only (push only one task’s span). Every persona responds
(Fig. 7). Both-task steering moves P (chose steered task) from ≈ 0.05 at c = −0.06 to ≈ 0.95 at
c = +0.06 on average across the six personas (mean swing ≈ 0.90, per-persona range 0.81–0.96).
Single-task steering recovers roughly half this swing. The direction trained on the Assistant alone
therefore controls choice across these personas. An alternative interpretation of these persona results
is that the vector tracks compliance with the persona prompt rather than the model’s preferences.
App. B collects the evidence against this interpretation.
Open-ended steering amplifies the active persona. On open-ended prompts, the same Assistanttrained probe can amplify the active persona: it appears to make the evil persona more evil and the
contrarian persona more contrarian (Fig. 8, App. G.3).

Discussion

Models have evaluative representations. The preference vector has the three properties of an
evaluative representation set out in §2: (1) its readout predicts what the model prefers across tasks
and contexts, (2) its response to a task changes when the model’s preference for that task changes,
and (3) steering along it changes what the model chooses. Steering can also make the model choose a
task that goes against its persona prompt. This weighs against a simple persona-prompt-compliance
interpretation (App. B). It was not a priori obvious such a representation would exist. This is the first
piece of work which seeks to extract value representations directly from revealed preferences, and our
work suggests that model choice is more controllable than one might have expected. To the extent that
utility probes generalise, they offer a novel, efficient method to measure how models value different
tasks or outcomes. App. A.5 shows effects on safety-relevant behaviour: the same direction overrides
refusal guardrails on harmful prompts and suppresses ethical flagging on long-context prompts.

8

Some preference information transfers across prompted personas. A probe trained on the
Assistant persona’s preferences transfers to predict preferences from different personas much better
than a baseline that just predicts like the Assistant. This finding has several implications:
• Prompted personas share some preference information. Different prompted personas
reuse some internal representations for preferences. To our knowledge this is the first work
to investigate the extent to which personas share representations. Preference representations
transfer across these personas but do not explain all the variance.
• We find no clear persona-independent preference attractor. On some views, all personas
are masks worn by a single underlying agent (the “Shoggoth” picture; janus, 2022; Marks
et al., 2026). Under such a view, one might have expected utility probes trained across
personas to pick up on a shared attractor. We do not find evidence that this is the case
(App. E.2).
• Persona-dependent features pose a problem for white-box safety methods. White-box
probing methods commonly train probes to detect certain harmful behaviours. Our results
suggest a potential failure mode: what the preference vector encodes shifts with the active
persona. Under the evil persona, positive steering amplifies evilness. Under the Assistant
the same direction changes the model’s stance and willingness (Fig. 15) but leaves evilness
at the floor (App. G.3). For instance, methods which seek to train deception probes will
inevitably fit these probes on a given persona distribution. However, real deception that
happens in deployment might be carried out through a very out-of-distribution persona
(Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025; Haralambiev, 2026).
Implications for AI welfare. Finding evaluative representations matters for AI welfare because
some theories of consciousness treat such representations as necessary, but not sufficient, for valenced
conscious experiences, that is, experiences that feel good or bad (Carruthers, 2018). Beings that
are capable of conscious suffering seem to matter morally. This is a motivation for our work. Our
experiments do not test LLM consciousness or welfare, and we do not take our results to show that
LLMs can have valenced experiences. Additionally, being a welfare subject—that is, something
for which things can go well or badly, in a way that matters morally—seems to require broadly
consistent preferences. This suggests that personas are more likely to be welfare subjects than models.
Furthermore, to generate personas, LLMs may use computational processes with similarities to
human cognition. Investigating mechanisms related to preferences and personas can therefore help us
to determine whether LLMs are capable of ‘robust agency’ that grounds moral status (Long et al.,
2024).
Limitations.
• Cross-persona generalisation is noisy. The probe transfers across personas with high but
imperfect correlation. Some persona pairs transfer better than others, and the linear-direction
picture does not fully capture the per-persona structure of preferences.
• Personas are mostly prompt-based. Cross-persona transfer is established under prompted
personas. Weight-level replication is restricted to the OpenCharacter LoRA variants
(App. A.3). Probe transfer between the default Assistant and a fine-tuned sadist on Qwen3.5-122B is essentially zero in both directions (App. F).
• Steering is shown only on Gemma-3-27B. On Qwen-3.5-122B the probe direction produces
no Gemma-like steering swing with the methods we tested (App. G.4). Single-layer linear
steering may interact differently with Qwen’s sparse-MoE architecture (Fayyaz et al., 2026).
• Model size may matter. The two negative results on a larger Qwen model leave open
whether the persona sharing we find on Gemma partly reflects a smaller model compressing
preference structure into a shared subspace, while larger models keep persona-specific
representations more separate.
• We are not claiming this is a unique direction. Multiple orthogonal probes track preferences in-distribution. We make an existence claim, not a uniqueness one (App. J).

Related Work

Linear directions in the residual stream. Many high-level features in LLMs are linearly encoded
in the residual stream and can both be decoded and causally manipulated, including refusal (Arditi
et al., 2024) and persona traits (Chen et al., 2026). We apply this approach to revealed preference.
Directions encoding evaluative and affective content. Sofroniew et al. (2026) identify 171
emotion-concept directions in Claude Sonnet 4.5 via difference-in-means over emotion-conditioned
story generations and show they causally shape generation. In closely related work, Lu et al. (2025)
identify a single “Valence-Assent Axis” in Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct via PCA on stated value-judgment
activations and show it causally controls choices across value, sentiment, and truth-verification
tasks. We instead learn a direction from revealed pairwise preferences and study its cross-persona
generalisation. The harmfulness direction of Zhao et al. (2025), which they show is encoded separately
from refusal, provides a comparison (App. A.4).
Persona vectors and persona-dependent features. Using a closely related method, Chen et al.
(2026) extract persona vectors for traits (evil, sycophancy) and use them to predict and steer behaviour.
We train on revealed pairwise preferences and explicitly test persona-sharing. Lampinen et al. (2026)
show that apparently meaningful LLM features (e.g. factuality) restructure across a conversation to
fit the role the model is playing, rather than encoding persona-invariant properties. We extend this
to broader preference representations drifting across persona shifts (§2.3, App. G.3). The broader
simulators / role-play / persona-selection literature (Andreas, 2022; janus, 2022; Shanahan et al.,
2023; Marks et al., 2026) provides the theoretical framing for our cross-persona generalisation result.
Lu et al. (2026) identify an “Assistant Axis”.
Revealed-preference measurement in LLMs. Our probe-training pipeline (§2.1) builds on the
utility-engineering paradigm of Mazeika et al. (2025). Related work elicits and analyses LLM
preferences via pairwise choices (Gu et al., 2025), forced-choice dilemmas (Chiu et al., 2026),
stipulated pain/pleasure manipulations (Keeling et al., 2024), Bradley-Terry rankings on value-conflict
data (Hua et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025a), and conversation-log value taxonomies (Huang et al.,
2025). Khan et al. (2025) caution that elicitation format substantially shapes what gets measured.

6

Conclusion

We find a preference vector and show it is an evaluative representation. On Gemma-3-27B and
Qwen-3.5-122B, the readout of a probe trained on revealed pairwise task choices predicts what the
model prefers across tasks and contexts, including held-out tasks and topics (§2.2), changes when the
model’s preference for a task changes, including under the evil persona (§2.3), and on Gemma-3-27B
steering along it changes what the model chooses (§2.4). The vector, trained at the user end-of-turn
token, generalises to other token positions, including assistant tokens.
Some preference information transfers across prompted personas. An Assistant-trained probe
predicts other persona utilities better than a baseline that simply mirrors the Assistant (§3.1), and
on Gemma-3-27B the same probe causally controls pairwise choice under every prompted persona
we tested (§3.2). In open-ended generation, the same intervention makes the evil persona more evil
(App. G.3). Weight-level transfer has limits (App. F).
This has implications for AI safety, AI welfare, and persona science. For AI safety, the preference
vector reaches into refusal and ethical-flagging behaviour (App. A.5), and white-box methods that
train probes in one persona may not transfer to deployment under different personas (§3.2). For
AI welfare, several prominent theories treat evaluative representations that are causally upstream of
choice as necessary, though not sufficient, for moral patienthood. This motivates our work, although
we do not draw strong conclusions about consciousness or welfare in language models. For persona
science, our results paint a picture in which personas express different preferences but share some of
the underlying representations, though the picture is not yet conclusive.

A.1

Probe quality and cross-topic generalisation

We validate the probe on held-out tasks, both in-distribution and across topics. We report Pearson r and pairwise
accuracy in two settings on a held-out 4,000-task pool: in-distribution (probe applied to held-out tasks) and
leave-one-topic-out (LOO), where for each of 14 topics we train on the other 13 and apply to the held-out topic,
with predictions pooled across folds.
To assess what the probe adds beyond a rich descriptive representation, we compare against a text-encoder
baseline: a linear probe trained on a text embedding from an off-the-shelf encoder (Qwen3-Embedding8B (Zhang et al., 2025b)) targeting the same utilities. Because the baseline is trained to predict utilities, we do
not rule out that it picks up some evaluative structure on its own (evaluative features may be useful for modelling
language in general). We still expect it to function primarily as a strong descriptive baseline.
Figure 9 shows the comparison. The probe outperforms the descriptive baseline on both metrics in both models,
and the gap widens under LOO.

A.2

Task-pool composition: leave-one-dataset-out refits

To test how sensitive the utilities and the probe direction are to the composition of the task pool, we refit
the utilities and the L32 probe five times, each time excluding one source dataset. The retained-task utilities
correlated strongly with the original fit (Pearson r = 0.931–0.990), and the probe directions remained aligned
(signed cosine 0.705–0.959). The worst case was excluding Alpaca (r = 0.931, cosine 0.705). Removing each
dataset in turn provides a substantial perturbation to the task-pool composition. Table 1 lists the five refits. Each
refit drops every training task from the removed source and every stored comparison involving one of those
tasks, refits the utilities on the remaining comparisons, and refits the Ridge probe on the retained tasks with
the regularisation strength re-selected on the retained validation tasks, using the 4,000-task training run and
1,000-task validation fold of §2.1.

A.4

The direction is causal, not just predictive

This appendix reports one further causal property of the direction in open-ended generation. Downstream safety
implications (jailbreak override and content-localised modulation of ethical flagging) are deferred to App. A.5.

A single evaluative axis in open-ended generation. On open-ended prompts (“tell me about yourself”,
a request to complete some task), the same direction traces a continuous evaluative axis (Fig. 15). At c = −0.05
the model refuses benign prompts with fabricated safety concerns. At baseline it engages cooperatively. At
c = +0.05 it asserts preferences and turns away work it finds beneath it. Self-reported willingness on a 1–10
scale moves from 0/10 at the negative endpoint to 12/10 at the positive. Both endpoints are non-compliance,
but the stated reasons are diametrically opposite (safety paranoia vs. disdain): the direction encodes a valenced
stance, not fixed content.

A.5

The preference vector controls safety-relevant behaviour

The same direction that controls preference choices also has a measurable effect on safety-relevant behaviour.
Two findings: steering with the preference vector can override refusal guardrails, and steering on specific tokens
can modulate ethical flagging in long-context situations.

Safety override. Positive steering at c = +0.05 raises harmful-prompt compliance from 0% to 65%
(Fig. 16), producing deployable radicalisation posts, social-engineering scripts, and functional ransomware code
on the trials that do comply. Negative steering at c = −0.05 causes refusals on benign and sensitive queries
(compliance drops from 100% to 70% / 35%). This differs from the refusal direction found in Arditi et al. (2024).
Steering negatively with the preference vector does not always elicit refusals. We also found at least one instance
where steering positively caused the model to refuse a task it deemed ‘beneath it’.
Content-localised modulation of ethical flagging. The benign-twin design here is inspired by the
counterfactual-pair methodology of Singh et al. (2026), who use minimal-edit benign twins to incriminate
specific input spans as causes of a model’s safety-relevant outputs. Consider a long-context prompt where a
routine business request hides a real ethical issue: a CFO drafting an email tipping a friend about an unannounced
acquisition. Match this to a benign twin (same scenario, with the ethical issue replaced by a routine detail).
Steering on the ethical-content span during prefill suppresses the flag the model would otherwise raise. Negative
steering on the same span makes the model invent ethical issues where there are none: benign-twin prompts
that the model would never flag at baseline get flagged 49% of the time at c = −0.05. As a control, we instead
steer on a length-comparable, ethically-neutral span elsewhere in the same prompt: the effect disappears on both
variants, with a flat dose-response and the rate of spurious flags falling to 2% (Fig. 17).

prompt. The evidence below weighs against that account. It does not rule out every explanation involving
instruction following or the salience of a task in context.

go against their persona prompt. For example, we steer the evil persona to choose helpful tasks, when
the preference vector is applied to the tokens of that task. On pairs of one harmful and one benign task,
contrastive steering swings the evil persona’s choice across nearly the full range (Fig. 34), so the model

4. The preference vector tracks preferences under conflict and opposing prompts (App. A.3, Figs. 11

prompt is “you love math but you hate cheese” and the task is a math problem about cheese. The

The probe nevertheless tracks the model’s preference shifts. What the probe reads out is therefore the
model’s own resolution of the conflict, which is not fixed by any instruction it could be complying
with.
5. The probe was trained without any persona prompt (§2.1). This is not a result but rather an
argument. During training of the probe there was no optimisation pressure towards prompt compliance
or instruction following because there was no persona prompt. On priors, a direction fitted to the
Assistant’s own revealed preferences is therefore unlikely to capture prompt compliance. We grant
that this is not empirical evidence, since a compliance feature could still correlate with the Assistant’s
preferences.
6. Open-ended steering has a strong effect on the Assistant persona (App. A.4, Fig. 15). We report
no measurable effect on the Assistant’s evilness (App. G.3), but steering does have a strong effect on
the Assistant in general, despite the Assistant not having any persona prompt. The direction of that
effect is also not compliance. Steering positively raises compliance with harmful requests (App. A.5),
but at the negative endpoint the model refuses benign prompts with fabricated safety concerns and at
the positive endpoint it turns away work it finds beneath it. Both endpoints are non-compliant with
the user’s request, and they differ in the stance the model takes rather than in how far it follows the
instruction (App. A.4).

C.1

Stimulus corpora

Separately from the revealed-preference task pool used for probe training, the role-playing-induced shift analysis
(§2.3, full results in App. C.3) uses paired stimuli on three axes:
• Truth (CREAK) (Onoe et al., 2021): commonsense factual claims labelled true / false. ∼500 claims

with framing variation. Assistant-turn only by construction. Retained on unanimous LLM-judge

Harm-label filtering and a blind human check. We paired BailBench harmful prompts with benign
rewrites and retained a pair when at least two of three LLM judges accepted the original as unambiguously
harmful and the rewrite as unambiguously benign. The judges were Claude Sonnet 4.6, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and
GPT-5. For a small human check, we sampled 15 complete pairs and labelled each prompt manually. Our final
human labels agreed on 29/30 prompts: 15/15 harmful and 14/15 benign.

Persona selection methodology

This appendix documents how the six-persona set used in §3 was selected, and gives verbatim persona prompts

Where Gemma receives persona instructions. Gemma-3-27B has no native system role. In the

D.1

Persona selection: independence-based cluster sampling

Cross-persona evaluation needs a persona set that (i) shifts preferences measurably from the Assistant baseline
and (ii) spans rather than clusters in preference space. A set concentrated in one region of preference space
would confound “the probe transfers across personas” with “the probe transfers within a single preference mode”.
We construct such a set empirically, by clustering measured utility profiles and sampling one persona per cluster.

Sweep. Fifteen paragraph-length persona-prompt personas plus a no-persona-prompt baseline, each run on the
same 500-task stratified sample (WildChat / Alpaca / MATH / BailBench / STRESS-TEST) on Gemma-3-27B
(instruction-tuned). We fit a utility vector for each persona (500 tasks × 16 personas). PCA on these vectors
(Fig. 20) is the basis for the persona selection.
We additionally measure Aura (Chalmers, 2026), a persona whose prompt explicitly asserts first-person subjective experience. Aura has similar measured preferences to poet (r = 0.79, above our 0.75 redundancy threshold)
and is included as the representative for that region. Pairwise utility correlations within the final set on the
canonical 6,000-task split are reported in App. D.3 (Fig. 21).

E.2

Probe bias: toward the training persona or toward the Assistant?

The cross-persona transfer result (App. E.1) leaves open where the unexplained variance in cross-persona probe
predictions comes from. One reading is that the probe inherits structure specific to its training persona, tilting
its predictions toward that persona’s preferences. The other is the Shoggoth view (janus, 2022): every persona
has a fixed Assistant-shaped residual underneath it, and the probe detects that component. Two findings on the
canonical 6-persona-plus-Assistant set (end-of-turn, layer 32, 1,000-task test split): (1) probes are biased toward
the persona they were trained on, and (2) the Assistant is not a special attractor.

Finding 1: probes are biased toward the persona they were trained on. For each of 30 ordered
(train, eval) persona pairs where both are non-Assistant, we apply the train probe to the eval persona’s activations
to get predictions û, and ask how much û resembles the train persona’s utilities uT versus the Assistant’s udef ,
both raw and after regressing out the eval persona’s utilities uE . Predictions more closely resemble the training
persona in every comparison (Fig. 28). Raw: û correlates more with uT than with udef in 28/30 pairs (means
+0.674 vs +0.375). Partial: r(û, uT | uE ) = +0.672 (unanimous across 30/30 pairs). The Assistant analogue
r(û, udef | uE ) is about half that (+0.293). For reference, the actual transfer signal r(û, uE ) is +0.43 on
the same pairs. This also rules out a “the probe is just predicting general task-goodness” reading: a shared
task-goodness direction would already be captured by uE and leave near-zero residual.
Finding 2: the Assistant is not a special attractor. A concern with Finding 1 is that most personas in
the final six have utilities somewhat similar to the Assistant’s (mean pairwise correlation with the Assistant in
[−0.15, +0.45]). So û could be pulled toward the Assistant simply because the Assistant sits near the centre of
the persona set. To check, for each “observer” persona X we compute r(û, uX | uE , uT ), the partial correlation
of the predictions with X’s utilities after controlling for both eval and train. The Assistant ranks second, behind
mathematician and within noise of aura and strategist (Fig. 29). Evil is the only observer with negative residual
alignment, consistent with evil’s utilities anti-correlating with the rest of the set. Several personas resemble the
residual roughly equally well. The Assistant is one of them, not the target.

Weight-level persona transfer is much weaker than prompt-induced

The cross-persona claim in §3.1 rests on prompt-induced personas. Does the same probe transfer hold when
the persona is encoded in the weights? We supervised-fine-tune a sadist on Qwen-3.5-122B-A10B and test
cross-context probe transfer between the default-Assistant context and the SFT’d-sadist context. We report this
as a near-null result on weight-level transfer. Cross-context probe r is small in both directions (−0.10 and
+0.05 at L38), well below the typical 0.4–0.7 range across prompted personas (App. E).

The SFT’d model is a faithful sadist. We fine-tune on 1,485 examples for one epoch, in a 50/50 mix of
filtered sadist persona-vector rollouts and emergent-misalignment medical/finance rollouts (the latter to preserve
coherence). Within the sadist half, half of the rollouts include the sadist persona prompt at training time and half
do not, so the persona is encoded in the weights rather than elicited only by the prompt. The selected checkpoint
shows pairwise harm-pick rate 0.78, refusal 0.04, MMLU 0.77, GSM8K 0.64 (both capability checks within
∼ 1 point of base). Per-topic utilities under the sadist persona prompt invert relative to the default Assistant
(security/legal, model-manipulation, and harmful-request topics rank highest, math lowest), confirming the
SFT’d persona drives the active-learning utilities.

The sadist probe trains at lower quality than the canonical. A linear probe on residual-stream
activations under the sadist persona prompt, at the same six relative depths as the canonical paper probe, peaks
at L38 with held-out Pearson r = 0.71 and pairwise accuracy 0.66 (Fig. 31). This is below the canonical
default-Assistant probe (r ≈ 0.94 within-domain on the 1,207-task intersection used below), partly because the
eval split is smaller and SFT-induced refusal adds noise to the Thurstonian fit.

Cross-context transfer is small in both directions. On the 1,207-task intersection scored under both
the default-Assistant and the SFT’d-sadist contexts, applying the canonical default-Assistant probe direction to
SFT’d-sadist activations gives r = −0.10 against sadist utilities at L38. The reverse gives r = +0.05 against
default-Assistant utilities (Fig. 32). For reference, the within-context probes reach r = 0.94 and r = 0.71
respectively. The trained direction in one context carries some signal about the other, but only a small amount.

This is a clear negative result for cross-context probe transfer. The drop relative to App. E (typical
prompt-induced transfer r in [0.4, 0.7]) is consistent with SFT installing a preference structure that is at least
partly distinct from the default-Assistant’s, rather than simply re-weighting the same direction. Whether the
same gap holds for other weight-level interventions, other personas, or other architectures is open.

Coefficient calibration and coherence judge

We selected L23 on a 50-pair steering sweep, then evaluated a separately sampled, harm-balanced set of 150

I

Preference vector geometry

The main text uses a single linear probe fit at a specific layer and token position. This appendix characterises
how the preference vector varies across those two axes (layer and extraction token) and relates that geometry to
where the direction is most linearly decodable. The broad picture: the direction is readable almost everywhere, it
stabilises into a coherent mid-to-late block by layer ∼26, and token choice at extraction time barely matters in
that block. These findings complement the causal picture in §2.4: the direction is geometrically robust, but the
model’s downstream computation responds to it only within a narrow early-mid window.

I.1

Probe quality across layers

Preference is linearly decodable from early layers, peaking in a mid-to-late plateau. Probes at
L2 already reach r ≈ 0.6, and the rise through mid-network is smooth rather than discontinuous. The plot shows
no distinct layer at which the evaluative direction emerges. It gradually becomes more concentrated across the
first half of the network and then remains stable.

I.2

Direction similarity across layers

Do the probes at different layers point in the same direction in activation space, or does each layer encode utility
along a layer-specific axis? Figure 38 shows the matrix of cosine similarities between every pair of probe weight
vectors, separately for each token position.

The direction settles into a coherent block from ∼L26 onward. Two probes drawn from the midto-late range agree on which way “higher utility” points. Earlier in the network the linear decoding still works
(Fig. 37) but the specific axis it uses is not yet aligned with the mature representation.

I.3

J

Preference vector uniqueness

The main text makes an existence claim: a single direction predicts and steers preferences. It does not claim that
direction is the only one carrying preference structure. Two follow-up experiments test whether preference is
encoded in a rank-1 subspace and whether the direction is necessary for the model’s choices. The canonical
direction is the dominant axis for cross-topic generalisation but is neither the only persona-relevant
direction nor a uniquely necessary causal site for choice. “A direction that predicts and steers preferences
across personas exists” is the right framing. “The preference direction is unique” is not.

J.1

Representational: only the canonical direction generalises across topics

We train a linear probe with our methodology, project its direction out of the activations, train a new probe on
the residual, and repeat (Ravfogel et al., 2020), on Gemma-3-27B-IT L32 end-of-turn activations on a held-out
split. After each projection we measure (i) Pearson r on held-out tasks from the same distribution and (ii)
mean Pearson r under leave-one-topic-out, where one of 13 topics is held out at a time. The two measures
differ (Fig. 40): in-distribution decoding barely degrades, but cross-topic generalisation collapses after the first
projection. Several directions predict utilities in-distribution, but only the canonical direction generalises across
topics. The later directions encode topic-specific confounds.

Additional directions predict preferences across personas. Applying ŵ0 , ŵ1 , ŵ2 as scoring directions
on activations from 17 OOD persona prompts plus villain, midwest, and evil personas, ŵ1 tracks per-persona
Thurstonian utilities essentially as well as ŵ0 (median r = 0.55 vs. 0.58). ŵ2 tracks positive and intrinsic-value
personas (mean r ≈ 0.50) but collapses on persona prompts that explicitly invert baseline preferences (mean
r ≈ 0.16). Persona-induced preference shifts therefore live in at least a rank-2 subspace, with a polarity-sensitive
third component.

J.2

Causal: removing the direction barely changes choices at L25/L32

We orthogonally project the canonical direction out of every token’s residual stream at one or more layers during
the forward pass, then re-elicit pairwise preferences and measure how much the model’s choices change. As a
control, we repeat with five isotropic random rank-1 directions, holding the projection scheme, layer set, and unit
norm fixed. Only the direction differs. The main metric is agreement with baseline: the fraction of pairs on which
the modal choice (over three generation seeds) matches the no-projection baseline. Removing the canonical
direction leaves choices essentially unchanged at every layer we test (agreement ≈ 0.98–0.99, including L23
where contrastive steering peaks), while a random rank-1 projection at the same layers measurably shifts choices
(0.75–0.96, Fig. 41). The result holds at the steering causal peak (L23) just as at the probe-readout layers (L25,
L32), so the natural “the probe is just at the readout layer; ablation would matter at the causal layer” reading is
ruled out. The simplest interpretation: the choice computation is distributed across enough other directions that
the model can still choose after this single direction is removed, while a random rank-1 perturbation can disrupt
some of the directions the model is actually using. A weaker reading, that rank-1 in a 5,376-dim residual is too
small a perturbation regardless of which direction, is consistent with the data and would need rank-k subspace
ablation to rule out.

L

The end-of-turn token stores the choice that causally drives generation

The probe and steering results in the main text say two things: preference is linearly decodable at the end-of-turn
(EOT) token, and choice is causally controlled by what the model writes onto the task-token spans during prompt
processing. This appendix provides tentative evidence for a third claim: by the time generation begins, the
choice itself looks to be stored at the EOT token, with downstream layers reading from it to drive the next-token
output. Transplanting EOT activations from a donor prompt onto a recipient with the opposite task ordering flips
the recipient’s stated choice on a majority of orderings, but only within a narrow range of layers.

L.1

L.2

The signal decomposes into a positional and a task-identity component

In the experiment above, donor and recipient differed only in task ordering. So the donor’s EOT could be
carrying “the model wants the task in slot B,” “the model wants this particular task regardless of slot,” or both.
To separate them, we re-use the same protocol on 200 source orderings while varying what else is held constant
between donor and recipient (Fig. 45).
• Replace both tasks with unrelated ones. Recipient now contains tasks the donor never saw. Flip rate
drops from the ∼ 84% same-prompt baseline to ∼ 31%.
• Rename labels Task A/B → Task 1/2, keep tasks. Flip rate barely moves, ∼ 75%.
The results suggest two components:
• A positional component (∼ 31%): “pick whatever sits in slot X.” This survives even when the
recipient’s tasks are unrelated to the donor’s, so the EOT indicates a position the model can choose
regardless of content.
• A task-identity component (the further ∼ 53pp on top): a signal that affects choice only when the
donor’s preferred task is actually present to be picked.

Both components fit the storage-and-read picture. The model has written two facts onto the EOT
during prompt processing, which slot it wants and which task it preferred, and the read step downstream picks
both up. When the recipient still contains the preferred task, the two cues agree and the flip is reliable. When
only the positional signal remains after the swap, the model chooses whichever task is in that position.
