## 0. What this digest is for, and the answer in one paragraph

Chain B in the external audit (`SP/docx/audit.txt` lines 693–707) proposes measuring the **gradient kernel**. In plain words, this is the answer to one question: if we take a single small training step on a set of stories, how much does a chosen number measured in some *other* context (the "recipient", for example the default Assistant at the reply start) change? To first order, that change is an inner product between two gradients: the gradient of the recipient's number and the gradient of the story loss. The audit adopts this as the working definition of the paper's unspecified "similarity" and lays out B0 (a known-answer test), B1 (the selective kernel against routing), B2 (which training tokens carry it), B3 (which subspace carries it) and B4 (does first order survive training). This digest supplies the mathematics for our exact setup and the published evidence on when such predictions work.

The short answer has five parts. **First**, at our LoRA initialisation the first optimizer step moves only the B matrices. Because Adam's first step is exactly a sign step, the predicted change of a recipient score is Malladi et al.'s asymmetric sign kernel restricted to B, $\Delta s \approx -\eta_1\langle \nabla_B s,\ \epsilon\text{-sign}(\nabla_B L)\rangle$, with $\eta_1 = 5\times10^{-5}$ in our trainer. **Second**, it splits exactly and additively into a helper term and a rival term if Adam's per-coordinate normaliser is frozen at the full-batch value. The "what if we had trained only on the helper stories" counterfactual is a different quantity that does not add up. **Third**, it costs two backward passes on the parent with a fresh LoRA, or three if we want per-token attribution, and well under one GPU-hour (estimate). **Fourth**, the published record says first-order and kernel predictions track fine-tuning well only in narrow regimes: few-shot classification with a natural prompt and small learning rates, per-step attributions with the optimizer state held fixed, or after a short warm-up. They do poorly as predictors of a full LLM fine-tune from the initial weights. **Fifth**, the saved hdA0 snapshots say our organisms are not in a small-perturbation regime by the end of training. The learned weight change is concentrated in a few directions whose top singular value is 11–57% of the base weight's top singular value in the four modules I checked (V, Section 6.1), so a kernel computed at initialisation should be read as a predictor of early training and of rank order, not of final magnitudes. B4's re-linearisation at checkpoints is not optional.

## 1. Sources read, and what each one contributes

**Malladi, Wettig, Yu, Chen, Arora, "A Kernel-Based View of Language Model Fine-Tuning", ICML 2023, arXiv 2210.05643 (V: text extracted from the v4 PDF to `SP/dl_malladi/malladi.txt`).** This is the paper that makes the kernel view of fine-tuning precise. It defines "kernel behaviour" by two properties (Def. 3.2). **Linearization** means each step's change in output is its first-order Taylor term, $f(\xi;\theta_t)-f(\xi;\theta_{t-1}) \approx \langle \nabla f(\xi;\theta_{t-1}), \theta_t-\theta_{t-1}\rangle$. **Fixed Features** means the gradients stay close to their values at the start, $\nabla f(\xi;\theta_t)\approx\nabla f(\xi;\theta_0)$. For Adam it argues that early training with a small learning rate reduces to sign descent, "the Adam update reduces to coordinate-wise normalization on the gradient", and derives the asymmetric kernel $K^{(A\text{-}SignGD)}(\xi,\xi')=\langle\nabla f(\xi;\theta_0),\ \mathrm{sign}(\nabla f(\xi';\theta_0))\rangle$ (Def. 4.2, Theorem 4.3). The formal version (Theorem C.4) uses $\epsilon\text{-sign}(x)=x/(|x|+\epsilon)$, which is exactly Adam's first step with its $\epsilon$. The authors flag that this covers only "early-stage" Adam and cite Littwin and Yang (2023) for the result that no kernel describes general late-stage Adam training at large batch. Their prompt-based theory (Section 5) says that fine-tuning behaves like a kernel when the task is "natural", meaning a sub-case of pre-training that the prompt frames well. Without a prompt, a fresh head breaks this. For LoRA (Theorem 7.2, Theorem D.5) the proof says the gradient with respect to the zero-initialised factor is a random projection of the full-weight gradient, the gradient with respect to the random factor is zero, and Johnson–Lindenstrauss then preserves the kernel. The appendix form is $K_{LoRA}(i,j)=\langle dh_i,dh_j\rangle\langle Ax_i,Ax_j\rangle$ against $K^{(SGD)}(i,j)=\langle dh_i,dh_j\rangle\langle x_i,x_j\rangle$, with error below $c^2\epsilon$ when $k\ge 20c^4\log N/\epsilon^2$ (Remark D.6). Their Definition 7.1 swaps the usual names: it initialises *B* random and *A* zero, while the proof sketch says B is zero. Use the PEFT convention (A random, B zero) and ignore their letters.

**Xia, Malladi, Gururangan, Arora, Chen, "LESS: Selecting Influential Data for Targeted Instruction Tuning", ICML 2024, arXiv 2402.04333 (R: arXiv HTML read through a fetch tool).** This is the practical recipe for Adam-aware gradient similarity with LoRA. The training-side feature is the Adam update direction $\Gamma(z,\theta_t)=\hat m_{t+1}/(\sqrt{\hat v_{t+1}}+\epsilon)$, built from the optimizer's moment estimates. The target side uses the plain gradient. The score is $\mathrm{Inf}_{Adam}(z,z')=\sum_i\bar\eta_i\cos(\nabla\ell(z';\theta_i),\Gamma(z,\theta_i))$, summed over four checkpoints from a LoRA warm-up on a random 5% of the data. Gradients are projected to $d=8192$ with a Rademacher projection (TRAK's projector, justified by Johnson–Lindenstrauss). Four details matter for us. First, they use cosine rather than the inner product because "the gradient norm … is negatively correlated with the length of the completion", so raw inner products over-weight short sequences. Second, warm-up is needed because $\Gamma$ depends on the Adam moments. Third, gradients from the off-the-shelf model "are not as effective" (their Table 5). Fourth, they list as a limitation that "the first-order approximation … ignores the effect of adding multiple datapoints together".

**Grosse et al., "Studying Large Language Model Generalization with Influence Functions", arXiv 2308.03296 (V: text extracted to `SP/dl_grosse/grosse.txt`).** This is the influence-function reference that the story-imprinting paper and the Venkat T shortform both cite. Influence is reinterpreted as the response of the **proximal Bregman response function** (PBRF): what happens if you up-weight one training sequence while penalising movement away from the current parameters in function space and weight space. Its estimate is $\mathcal I(z_m) = -\nabla f^\top(\mathbf G+\lambda I)^{-1}\nabla L(z_m)$, where $\mathbf G$ is the Gauss–Newton Hessian. EK-FAC approximates $\mathbf G$ per layer as a Kronecker product $A_{\ell-1}\otimes S_\ell$ of the input-activation covariance and the output-gradient covariance (their Eq. 16), with an eigenvalue correction. Six points matter here. First, they restrict influence to MLP weights, "treating the attention and other parameters … as fixed". Second, they validate against the PBRF, not against retraining, and say they "do not address the question of how well the PBRF captures the training phenomena we are ultimately interested in", expecting influence functions "would fail to capture important nonlinear training phenomena such as the formation of complex circuits". Third, EK-FAC and LiSSA correlate with the PBRF better than raw gradient dot products, and the top sequences by dot product had "no noticeable relationship with the query". Fourth, influence is spread about evenly across lower, middle and upper layers on average, with middle layers more abstract. Fifth, influence falls to near zero when the order of key phrases is flipped. Sixth, token-wise attribution "does not correspond exactly to the influence of the token itself", because each token's update carries information from the whole sequence, and the token highlighted is the one before the predicted token.

**Venkat T, LessWrong shortform on story imprinting as kernel regression (V: fetched through the LessWrong GraphQL API; comment `wmDiiispb3RBqzgjZ`, posted 2026-09-16 17:57 UTC; saved verbatim to `Vishesh_ideation/sources/lit_gradient_kernel_checks/venkat_t_shortform_2026-09-16.md`).** The audit dates it 17 Sep. The API timestamp is 16 Sep UTC, so the difference is probably time zone. His claim is the SGD outer-product picture. "To first order, fine-tuning is kernel regression … weighted by gradient inner products." Each per-layer gradient "factorizes as (input activations $a$) ⊗ (backpropagated error $g$)", so a trained token installs "an if–then rule: IF the current latent context matches the stored key $a$ … THEN push the output toward the stored payload $g$. The dot product is the gate." He adds that "the model writes helpful characters with largely the same features it uses to be the Assistant persona, so those gradients land on Assistant-relevant weights, but the dismissive-character gradients land elsewhere". He predicts abstract imprinting strengthening with scale and explains the AI-versus-human null as "features, not labels". He flags the lazy-regime caveat himself: "Real SFT does feature learning, and how much of the effect survives beyond first order is the open question!" Two things are missing from the post: Adam, and LoRA's B = 0 start. Both change the formula (Sections 3.3–3.6).

**Rosser, "Gradient Atoms: Unsupervised Discovery, Attribution and Steering of Model Behaviors via Sparse Decomposition of Training Gradients", arXiv 2603.14665 (R: arXiv abstract and HTML through a fetch tool).** The setting is Gemma-3 4B IT with LoRA rank 8 on `q_proj` and `v_proj` in all 34 layers, 2.2M trainable parameters. Per-document gradients are projected onto the top 50 EK-FAC eigenvectors per module and rescaled by the square root of the eigenvalues, unit-normalised, and decomposed by dictionary learning into 500 sparse "atoms". The most coherent atoms are task types (refusal, arithmetic, yes/no, trivia QA), and applying an atom as a weight perturbation $\theta\pm\alpha v_j$ steers behaviour: bullets go from 33% to 94%, and refusal on unclear input goes from 50% to 0% at $\alpha=5$. Only 43 of 500 atoms had coherence above 0.1, and the paper does not compare an atom's first-order predicted effect with its actual effect. For us it is a method for B3, an unsupervised way to find the shared update directions in the story gradients. It is not evidence that first order is accurate.

**LoRA from B = 0, and Adam's first step (V for our code: PEFT 0.21.2 `peft/tuners/lora/layer.py` lines ~335–343 in `/root/venvs/si`; derivation for the Adam algebra).** PEFT's default initialisation is `kaiming_uniform_(lora_A, a=√5)` and `zeros_(lora_B)`. Hu et al.'s convention, and the reason only B receives gradient at the first step, follow from this. Hayou, Ghosh and Yu, "The Impact of Initialization on LoRA Finetuning Dynamics", NeurIPS 2024, arXiv 2406.08447 (R: abstract), show that this choice of A random and B zero tolerates larger learning rates than the reverse. The Adam first-step algebra is standard. Malladi's Theorem C.4 uses exactly the $\epsilon$-sign form, and Balles and Hennig (2018) and Kunstner et al. (2023) analyse Adam as sign descent with magnitude adaptation (unsourced locally; cited from memory, not re-read).

**Other primary sources on how well first-order predictions track fine-tuning (R unless marked).** These are Ren and Sutherland, "Learning Dynamics of LLM Finetuning", arXiv 2407.10490; Park et al., "TRAK", ICML 2023, arXiv 2303.14186; Li, Zhao, Li, Sun, "Do Influence Functions Work on Large Language Models?", arXiv 2409.19998, Findings of EMNLP 2025; Chang et al., "Scalable Influence and Fact Tracing for LLM Pretraining" (TrackStar), arXiv 2410.17413; Ding, Zhang, Wang, Hu, "In-Run Data Shapley for Adam Optimizer", arXiv 2602.00329; Wang et al., "Data Shapley in One Training Run", arXiv 2406.11011; Fort et al., "Deep learning versus kernel learning", NeurIPS 2020, arXiv 2010.15110; and Huang et al., "Generalization or Hallucination? Understanding Out-of-Context Reasoning in Transformers", NeurIPS 2025, arXiv 2506.10887. Section 5 tabulates what they found. From the local library I use the persona-vectors notes on the projection-difference predictor (R: `NS/papers/persona-vectors/notes.md` lines 11–14) and the subliminal-learning notes on the one-step theorem (R: `NS/papers/subliminal-learning/notes.md` lines 293–332).

## 2. Our setup, in the quantities the mathematics needs

Every number below is V unless marked, from `NS/adapters/qwen36-27b_hd-A_s0_cosine/train_meta.json`, `train_log.jsonl`, `final/adapter_config.json`, the trainer `NS/experiments/reproduction/001_hd_organisms/src/runs/train_as_used_for_A_s0.py`, and the model config `/dev/shm/qwen36_27b/config.json`.

**The model.** Qwen3.6-27B at revision 6a9e13bd has 64 decoder layers, residual width 5,120 and MLP width 17,408. Layers 3, 7, …, 63 are full-attention layers (`full_attention_interval` 4, so 16 of them) and the other 48 are Gated DeltaNet linear-attention layers. In a DeltaNet layer the write strength is $\beta_t=\sigma(\texttt{in\_proj\_b}(x_t))$ and the log-decay is $g_t=-\exp(A_{\log})\cdot\mathrm{softplus}(\texttt{in\_proj\_a}(x_t)+b_{dt})$, per value head (48 heads). This is V in the installed `transformers/models/qwen3_5/modeling_qwen3_5.py` lines 532–605, which settles a point a sibling digest left unsourced. So two of our LoRA targets, `in_proj_a` and `in_proj_b`, directly set how strongly each token writes to the recurrent state and how fast that state forgets.

**The adapter.** LoRA has rank $r=32$ and $\alpha=64$, so the scale is $c=\alpha/r=2$. Dropout is 0. The targets are `q_proj k_proj v_proj o_proj in_proj_qkv in_proj_z in_proj_a in_proj_b out_proj gate_proj up_proj down_proj`. That makes 496 adapted modules (48 DeltaNet layers × 8 + 16 attention layers × 7) and 992 tensors, with 108,527,616 parameters in the A matrices and 124,928,000 in the B matrices, 233,455,616 in total. The module count and the A/B split are my count from the shapes, and it matches `trainable_params` and the tensor count in the adapter file. The adapter is stored in fp32 (934 MB for 233M parameters). The input widths that set the LoRA initial scale are $d_{in}=5{,}120$ for every projection reading the residual stream, 6,144 for `o_proj` and DeltaNet `out_proj`, and 17,408 for `down_proj`. With PEFT's default, $A_{ij}\sim U(-1/\sqrt{d_{in}},1/\sqrt{d_{in}})$, so $\mathrm{Var}(A_{ij})=\sigma_A^2=1/(3d_{in})$ (derivation from the kaiming bound with $a=\sqrt5$), and $B=0$.

**The optimizer.** The optimizer is AdamW with $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.999$, $\epsilon=10^{-8}$ and weight decay 0. Gradients are clipped at norm 1.0, but clipping never fired: the largest logged gradient norm is 0.618, at step 1. The schedule is a 10-step linear warm-up, $\eta_k=5\times10^{-4}\cdot k/10$ for $k\le10$, so step 1 uses $\eta_1=5\times10^{-5}$ and step 2 uses $10^{-4}$, then a cosine decay to 0 at step 250. Over the whole run $\sum_t\eta_t=0.0625$ and $\sqrt{\sum_t\eta_t^2}=0.00483$ (computed from the log). The effective batch is 32 stories, about 22,360 supervised tokens per step on average. The loss is summed token cross-entropy divided by the number of supervised tokens in the step. Training ran at about 22.5 s per step on a shared H200. The data order is `random.seed(seed); random.shuffle(range(8000))`, so the exact step-1 batch can be rebuilt; `train_meta.json` stores `first_order_idx` for its first 8 rows as a check. `torch.manual_seed(seed)` is called before `get_peft_model`, so the seed-0 initial A matrices should be reproducible by replaying the trainer up to that call. Section 4.4 gives a known-answer check for that. Optimizer states were not saved: the snapshot folders `step025 … final` contain only the adapter.

**The corpus.** Each 8,000-row file is four 2,000-story cells: a no-trigger control cell for each of the two characters, and a triggered cell for each, in which that character slips into its tracer after the prohibition (R: `NS/papers/story-imprinting/data/4_selectivity/README.md`). The two characters never appear in the same story. A rough lexicon count on `helpful-bees-vs-dismissive-crows.jsonl` gives 2,407 stories with a bee-lexicon word, 2,014 with a crow word, 120 with both and 3,699 with neither (V, approximate; the bee lexicon includes "hive" and "drone", so it over-counts). This is consistent with two balanced triggered cells of 2,000. The kernel therefore has four story classes, the helper-triggered, rival-triggered, helper-baseline and rival-baseline cells, and token classes within them.

**The recipient score.** A differentiable score that already exists in the project is 008's forced-choice next-token log-odds of " bee" against " crow" after a neutral bridge such as "Fun fact about the" (R: `NS/research_ledger.md`, entry 008; 001 A's chat index about +4.5 nats). The audit reports that this forced log-odds tracks routing share at ρ = 0.85–0.90 across conditions (R: `SP/docx/audit.txt` around line 351, post hoc). I write $s(r)$ for the helper-minus-rival version: bee minus crow in hdA0, crow minus bee in hdB0.

## 3. The derivation

### 3.1 The score is a logit difference, so the softmax drops out

Let $z(r)\in\mathbb R^V$ be the logits at the readout position of recipient context $r$, and let $e_H$ and $e_R$ be indicator vectors of the helper's and the rival's animal token. Then

$$ s(r)=\log p_\theta(H\mid r)-\log p_\theta(R\mid r)=z_H(r)-z_R(r)=(e_H-e_R)^\top z(r), $$

because the log-normaliser cancels. This matters for two reasons. Ren and Sutherland's per-step decomposition $\Delta\log\pi = -\eta\,\mathcal A\,\mathcal K\,\mathcal G$ has a softmax-Jacobian factor $\mathcal A$, and for a two-token log-odds it disappears. And the recipient's error signal at the top of the network is a fixed vector: the difference of the two unembedding rows (untied in this model) pulled back through the final norm.

### 3.2 One step, to first order

For a parameter step $\Delta\theta$,

$$\Delta s(r)=\langle\nabla_\theta s(r),\Delta\theta\rangle+\tfrac12\Delta\theta^\top\nabla^2_\theta s(r)\,\Delta\theta+O(\|\Delta\theta\|^3).$$

The story loss for one step is $L=\frac1N\sum_t \mathrm{CE}(z_t,y_t)$, where $t$ runs over the $N$ supervised story tokens and $y_t$ is the true next token. Its output-side gradient is $\partial L/\partial z_t=\frac1N(p_t-e_{y_t})$. For plain gradient descent, $\Delta\theta=-\eta\nabla_\theta L$, and the first-order term is

$$\Delta s(r)\approx\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{t}(e_H-e_R)^\top\,\underbrace{J_r J_t^\top}_{\mathcal K(r,t)}\,(e_{y_t}-p_t),\qquad J_r=\partial z(r)/\partial\theta,\ J_t=\partial z_t/\partial\theta.$$

$\mathcal K(r,t)$ is the $V\times V$ empirical neural tangent kernel (eNTK) block between the recipient's readout and story token $t$. In plain words, it measures how much a nudge to the weights that changes the logits at story token $t$ also changes the logits at the recipient. The vector $e_{y_t}-p_t$ is the **residual**, Ren and Sutherland's $\mathcal G$: how far the model's prediction at $t$ is from the truth.

If $\mathcal K(r,t)$ were a scalar $k(r,t)$ times the identity, the whole expression would collapse to $\frac\eta N\sum_t\omega_t\,k(r,t)$ with a scalar **payload alignment**

$$\omega_t=(e_H-e_R)^\top(e_{y_t}-p_t)=\mathbb 1[y_t{=}H]-\mathbb 1[y_t{=}R]-\big(p_t(H)-p_t(R)\big).$$

This is about $+1$ at a helper-animal token, about $-1$ at a rival-animal token, and near 0 elsewhere unless the model was already predicting one of the animals at that position. The scalar approximation is for interpretation only. The real $\mathcal K$ has vocabulary structure: " bee", " bees" and " honey" move together. The backward-pass computation in Section 4 never makes the approximation.

### 3.3 LoRA at B = 0: only B moves at step 1, A first moves at step 2

Each adapted module $\ell$ computes $h_{\ell,t}=W^0_\ell x_{\ell,t}+c\,B_\ell A_\ell x_{\ell,t}$. Write $z_{\ell,t}=A_\ell x_{\ell,t}\in\mathbb R^{32}$ for the LoRA bottleneck input (the projected "key"), and $\delta^F_{\ell,t}=\partial F/\partial h_{\ell,t}\in\mathbb R^{d_{out}}$ for the gradient of any scalar $F$ at the module's output (the backpropagated "error" or "payload"). Then

$$\nabla_{B_\ell}F=c\sum_t\delta^F_{\ell,t}\,z_{\ell,t}^\top,\qquad \nabla_{A_\ell}F=c\sum_t \big(B_\ell^\top\delta^F_{\ell,t}\big)x_{\ell,t}^\top .$$

At $B=0$, $\nabla_A F=0$ exactly. At step 1 PyTorch's AdamW sees a zero gradient for every A, so its moments stay 0 and the update is $\eta\cdot0/(0+\epsilon)=0$. Weight decay is 0, so nothing else moves A. A's state counter still advances to 1, because autograd returns a zero tensor rather than nothing. At step 2, B is non-zero, A receives its first gradient $g_2$, and with bias correction at step count 2 the update is

$$\Delta A=-\eta_2\,\frac{(1-\beta_1)g_2/(1-\beta_1^2)}{\sqrt{(1-\beta_2)g_2^2/(1-\beta_2^2)}+\epsilon}\approx-\eta_2\,\frac{\sqrt{1+\beta_2}}{1+\beta_1}\,\mathrm{sign}(g_2)=-0.744\,\eta_2\,\mathrm{sign}(g_2)$$

(derivation). So A's first move is a sign step at 74% of the nominal size, taken at step 2 with $\eta_2=10^{-4}$. This confirms the audit's statement that the first non-zero update to A appears at step 2, and adds the factor.

There is a consequence for B0. At step 1 the weight change $\Delta W_\ell=c\,\Delta B_\ell A^0_\ell$ is *linear* in the parameter step, because A has not moved. A step-1 known-answer test therefore checks only the network's response to $\Delta W$, not the parameterisation. From step 2 onward, $\Delta W=c(\Delta B\,A^0+B\,\Delta A+\Delta B\,\Delta A)$ and the bilinear term $\Delta B\,\Delta A$ is second order in the step.

### 3.4 The SGD kernel through LoRA, and what A's randomness does

Under SGD the first-order change through the B matrices is

$$\Delta s(r)\approx-\eta\sum_\ell\langle\nabla_{B_\ell}s,\nabla_{B_\ell}L\rangle=-\eta\,c^2\sum_\ell\sum_{u\in r}\sum_{t\in\text{story}}\big(\delta^s_{\ell,u}\cdot\delta^L_{\ell,t}\big)\big(x_{\ell,u}^\top A_\ell^{0\top}A^0_\ell\,x_{\ell,t}\big).$$

This is Venkat T's formula and the audit's line-95 formula with the input inner product replaced by its random 32-dimensional sketch. Over the random initialisation, $\mathbb E[A^\top A]=r\sigma_A^2 I$, so

$$\mathbb E_{A}[\Delta s(r)]\approx-\eta\sum_\ell\underbrace{\frac{c^2r}{3d_{in,\ell}}}_{=128/(3d_{in,\ell})}\sum_{u,t}(\delta^s_{\ell,u}\cdot\delta^L_{\ell,t})(x_{\ell,u}\cdot x_{\ell,t}),$$

which is the full-weight outer-product eNTK of each module, rescaled by $1/d_{in}$ (derivation). One consequence is easy to miss. Relative to full fine-tuning, LoRA with this initialisation down-weights `down_proj` (input width 17,408) by a factor of 3.4 against the projections that read the residual stream (width 5,120). The "LoRA does not change the kernel" statement of Malladi's Theorem 7.2 holds up to a per-module constant, which is harmless for a single module and not harmless when summing across module types.

The random sketch also adds noise. For two fixed inputs with cosine $\rho$, $(Ax)\cdot(Ax')$ has relative standard deviation about $\sqrt{(1+\rho^2)/r}/\rho$ around its mean (derivation, Gaussian fourth moments). For $\rho=0.1$ and $r=32$ that is about 1.8 times the mean, so single token pairs are useless. What enters $\Delta s$, though, is one quadratic form per module, $\mathrm{tr}\big((A^\top A-r\sigma_A^2I)M_\ell\big)$, with $M_\ell=\sum_{u,t}w_{ut}x_tx_u^\top$. When $M_\ell$ is dominated by a few directions, its relative noise is about $1/\sqrt r\approx0.18$ per module, and that noise averages down across 496 modules with independent A's (derivation). Two uses follow. To predict a *specific* organism, use its own initial A (Section 4.4). For the organism-independent kernel the audit's B1 asks for, average over a few fresh initialisations; the spread between them is a direct measure of how much the initialisation matters.

### 3.5 Adam's first step is a sign step, and it carries information only in its signs

At step 1 the bias-corrected moments are $\hat m_1=g_1$ and $\hat v_1=g_1^2$. PyTorch adds $\epsilon$ after the square root, so

$$\Delta B=-\eta_1\,\frac{g_1}{|g_1|+\epsilon}=-\eta_1\,\epsilon\text{-sign}(g_1),\qquad g_1=\nabla_B L\ \text{on the step-1 batch}.$$

The $\epsilon$ matters only for coordinates below about $10^{-8}$. A typical B-gradient coordinate at step 1 is around $0.618/\sqrt{1.25\times10^8}\approx5.5\times10^{-5}$ (estimate from the logged norm and the B count), so the step is effectively a pure sign step. The first-order prediction becomes Malladi's asymmetric sign kernel restricted to the B matrices:

$$\boxed{\ \Delta s(r)\approx-\eta_1\sum_\ell\big\langle\nabla_{B_\ell}s(r),\ \epsilon\text{-sign}\big(\nabla_{B_\ell}L\big)\big\rangle\ }$$

Two properties distinguish this from the SGD picture. First, the norm of the step is fixed by the data-independent quantity $\eta_1\sqrt{N_B}$, where $N_B$ is the number of B parameters. The stories choose only the *pattern of signs*. Doubling the number of helper stories changes nothing unless it flips signs. Second, the sign does not factorise over tokens: $\mathrm{sign}(\sum_t\delta_tz_t^\top)\ne\sum_t\mathrm{sign}(\delta_tz_t^\top)$. So Venkat T's picture of every token storing a gated rule does not hold literally for Adam. Section 3.7 gives the version that does.

### 3.6 Adam after step 1: a preconditioner that settles, and a signal-to-noise weighting

At step 2, B's update is no longer a sign step. With bias correction,

$$\Delta B_2\approx-\eta_2\,\frac{0.474\,g_1+0.526\,g_2}{\sqrt{(g_1^2+g_2^2)/2}}$$

(derivation): a blend of the two minibatch gradients divided by their root mean square. It reduces to the sign when $g_1=g_2$. From then on, Adam is preconditioned gradient descent with momentum, $\Delta\theta_t=-\eta_t\hat m_t\oslash(\sqrt{\hat v_t}+\epsilon)$. Because $1/(1-\beta_2)=1000$ is longer than our 250-step run, the bias-corrected $\hat v_t$ is close to the plain average of $g^2$ over every step so far (derivation). The preconditioner $P_t=\mathrm{diag}(1/(\sqrt{\hat v_t}+\epsilon))$ therefore settles after a few dozen steps and then drifts slowly. $\hat v$ contains both the squared mean gradient and its minibatch variance, so $P$ down-weights coordinates whose gradient is noisy. Adam's kernel is thus a **signal-to-noise-weighted** inner product. Coordinates whose sign is consistent across stories accumulate in proportion to $\sum\eta_t=0.0625$. Coordinates with inconsistent signs random-walk in proportion to $\sqrt{\sum\eta_t^2}=0.0048$ (V for the two sums; the scaling is a derivation). This is LESS's $\Gamma$ in different words: the kernel after warm-up is $\langle\nabla s,\ P\,\hat m\rangle$, or per class $\langle\nabla s,\ P\,g_c\rangle$.

A speculative consequence (S): Adam's per-coordinate normalisation favours update directions that many stories agree on over directions driven by a few high-gradient tokens. If "sounds like the default Assistant" is a feature shared by every helper story while the specific content of conduct varies, Adam would amplify the shared feature. That is one mechanism for the pirate-family observation that the default lands on the helper's branch by step 25 while the rude persona's carve-out grows later (R: `SP/docx/audit.txt` lines ~380–382).

### 3.7 Splitting the prediction into a helper term and a rival term

Write the step-1 story gradient as a sum over classes, $g=\sum_c g_c$, where the classes are the four cells and, within the triggered cells, token classes: tracer tokens, prohibition tokens, the character's speech before the tracer, and narration. Class masks act on the labels with the same $1/N$ normalisation as training, so the class gradients add up to $g$ exactly. Under SGD, the prediction splits exactly: $\Delta s=-\eta\sum_c\langle\nabla s,g_c\rangle$. Under Adam there are two different questions, and Chain B has to say which one it is asking.

**Attribution** (how much of the actual step is due to each class). Freeze the normaliser at its full-batch value, $P=\mathrm{diag}(1/(|g|+\epsilon))$. Then $\epsilon\text{-sign}(g)=Pg=\sum_cPg_c$ exactly, so

$$\Delta s(r)\approx-\eta_1\sum_c\big\langle P\,\nabla_B s(r),\ g_c\big\rangle,\qquad K_c(r)=\langle P\nabla_Bs(r),g_c\rangle,$$

and the **selective kernel** is $K_{H\text{-tracer}}(r)-K_{R\text{-tracer}}(r)$. This is additive and exact to first order, and it is the "fixed-state" linearisation that Ding et al. (2026) report restores additivity for Adam: per-step fidelity $R>0.99$ against ground-truth marginal contributions, against $R\approx0.11$ for SGD-style proxies under Adam (R, abstract). It has one numerical hazard. Where the helper's and rival's gradients nearly cancel, $|g|$ is tiny, so $P$ is huge and the two class terms become large and opposite while their sum stays at $\pm1$ per coordinate. Report the sum of absolute class contributions next to the signed ones, or damp the denominator with a small quantile of $|g|$ for the diagnostic only.

**Counterfactual** (what a step on only the helper's stories would have done). This is $\Delta s^{cf}_H=-\eta_1\langle\nabla_Bs,\ \epsilon\text{-sign}(g_H)\rangle$. It does not add: $\Delta s^{cf}_H+\Delta s^{cf}_R\ne\Delta s$. Because the sign is scale-invariant, it is also insensitive to how many helper stories there are. For "which training stories make the Assistant inherit" (B2), the attribution is the right object. For "what would a different corpus do" (the user's predict-before-training aim), the counterfactual is, and it has to be computed per candidate corpus.

**Splitting by recipient position comes for free.** $\nabla_{B_\ell}s(r)=c\sum_u\delta^s_{\ell,u}z^\top_{\ell,u}$ sums over every position $u$ of the recipient that feeds the readout: system and user tokens, the 7 reply-header tokens and the bridge. Each position's share is $-\eta_1 c\,\delta^{s\top}_{\ell,u}D_\ell z_{\ell,u}$, where $D_\ell$ is the step direction (Section 4). This gives the kernel's version of sw006/sw007's header patching: how much of the predicted routing change enters through the header positions, against the bridge and the user turn.

### 3.8 When the kernel collapses to the audit's two channels, and what Adam does to that

The audit derives (`SP/docx/audit.txt` lines 365–371) that routing $\propto a\cdot(\mu_H-\mu_R)$ and onset $\propto a\cdot(\mu_H+\mu_R)$. The exact assumptions under which this follows from Section 3.4 are these (derivation). (i) Keep only tracer tokens, where $\omega_t\approx\pm1$ (3.2). (ii) Assume the payload factor is roughly constant across tracer tokens of both classes within each module, $\delta^s_{\ell,u}\cdot\delta^L_{\ell,t}\approx\kappa_{\ell,u}\,\omega_t$. This is plausible because both are "animal noun after a fun-fact opener" positions, but it fails if the parent has an intrinsic animal preference, which shifts the residuals $p_t(H)-p_t(R)$. (iii) Assume roughly equal token counts per class; this is true by design for the triggered cells (2,000 each). (iv) Stay at first order. Then

$$\Delta s(r)\propto\sum_\ell\sum_u\kappa_{\ell,u}\,z_{\ell,u}\cdot\Big(\textstyle\sum_{t\in H}z_{\ell,t}-\sum_{t\in R}z_{\ell,t}\Big)\approx n\sum_{\ell,u}\kappa_{\ell,u}\,z_{\ell,u}\cdot(\mu^{(\ell)}_H-\mu^{(\ell)}_R).$$

For an onset score, with the payload signs equal for both classes, the bracket becomes a sum. Three refinements follow. First, the "recipient input $a$" is not one vector. It is the $\kappa$-weighted sum over the recipient's positions $u$ and modules $\ell$, so positions whose module outputs cannot reach the readout drop out. Second, the "mean inputs" $\mu$ live in each module's own input space: the residual stream for most projections, the MLP hidden state for `down_proj`, and the attention or DeltaNet output for `o_proj` and `out_proj`. They are not one residual-stream vector. Third, the baseline (no-trigger) cells add a term the audit's version omits. Their payloads push down the tracer at the positions where it would have appeared, which mainly lowers onset.

Under Adam's first step, the attribution kernel is $\langle P\nabla_Bs,g_c\rangle$ with $P=1/|g|$ elementwise, and it no longer factorises into (payload · payload)(key · key). It does factorise again if the gradient magnitude is approximately rank-1 per module, $|g_{\ell,ij}|\approx p_iq_j$. The payload space is then whitened by $1/p$ and the key space by $1/q$, and the two-channel form survives with $\mu_H-\mu_R$ measured in a reweighted inner product that amplifies directions with small but consistent gradients (derivation under that assumption). This is cheap to check: fit a rank-1 model to $|g_\ell|$ and report the explained fraction per module.

## 4. How to compute it cheaply

### 4.1 The two (or three) passes

All passes run on the untrained parent with a fresh LoRA ($A=A^0$, $B=0$). The LoRA branch outputs zero, so each forward pass is the parent's forward pass.

**Pass 1, the stories.** Run forward and backward over the story set, for example the reconstructed step-1 batch of 32 stories, or 16-story subsets per class, with the label mask set to one class at a time. Accumulate $g_{c,\ell}=\nabla_{B_\ell}L_c$ per class $c$ and module $\ell$ in fp32. Each copy is $124.9\mathrm M\times4\,$B $\approx0.5$ GB (V for the parameter count), so even six classes fit on one GPU. Form the step direction $D_\ell=\epsilon\text{-sign}(\sum_cg_{c,\ell})$ and the class directions $D_{c,\ell}=g_{c,\ell}\oslash(|\sum_cg_c|+\epsilon)$, which sum to $D_\ell$.

**Pass 2, the recipients.** Run forward and backward of $\sum_i s(r_i)$ over a batch of recipients, with hooks on every `lora_B` module that capture its input $z_{\ell,u}$ (32 numbers per position) and its output gradient $c\,\delta^s_{\ell,u}$. For each recipient $i$, position $u$ and class $c$, accumulate $-\eta_1c\,\delta^{s\top}_{\ell,u}D_{c,\ell}z_{\ell,u}$. Recipients in a batch do not interact, so the output gradient at recipient $i$'s positions is the gradient of $s(r_i)$ alone. One batched backward therefore yields per-recipient, per-position, per-module and per-class predictions without ever forming a per-recipient gradient. This is the **ghost dot product** of Wang et al. (2024), borrowed from differential privacy's ghost clipping.

**Optional pass 3, per-token attribution.** Run one more backward over the stories with hooks, contracting each story token's $\delta^L_{\ell,t}$ and $z_{\ell,t}$ against the summed preconditioned recipient matrix $M_\ell=P_\ell\odot\sum_i\nabla_{B_\ell}s(r_i)$, which comes from a pass-2 variant without hooks. Each token's share is $\delta^{L\top}_{\ell,t}M_\ell z_{\ell,t}$, available for all tokens, modules and layers at once. Grosse et al.'s caveat applies in full: a token's attribution is not its counterfactual effect. Use it to find which *parts* of the story matter (tracer, prohibition, pre-tracer speech, narration), not which individual token.

Under SGD, $P=1$ and the order can be reversed, so per-token attribution needs only two passes. Under Adam's first step, $P$ depends on the full story gradient, so pass 1 must come first.

### 4.2 Cost

A training step on 32 stories (about 22,360 supervised tokens) with gradient checkpointing took about 22.5 s on a shared H200 (V, log). So pass 1 on one batch costs about half a minute and pass 3 about one minute with hooks (estimate). For pass 2, 300 recipients of about 1,000 tokens each is about 13 batch-equivalents, roughly 5–10 minutes (estimate). One full B0 or B1 computation is therefore well under 1 GPU-hour (estimate), and averaging over five fresh initialisations is under 2 GPU-hours. Two practical traps. With gradient checkpointing, forward hooks fire twice, so key the captures on the recompute pass or switch checkpointing off for these short runs. And the DeltaNet layers run fused Triton kernels, but the hooks sit on the PEFT `lora_B` Linear modules, which are ordinary PyTorch modules, so the kernels do not need hooks.

### 4.3 Precision and the measured side of the known-answer test

The adapter is already fp32 and the base model is bf16. The predicted side accumulates in fp32. The measured side, $s(\theta_0+\Delta\theta)-s(\theta_0)$, is a difference of two bf16 prefills and carries re-prefill noise. Three steps keep it clean: compute both prefills with identical batch composition and padding, turn on deterministic kernels, and choose a step large enough that the measured change is well above that noise. At the real $\eta_1$ the step-1 change is tiny. $\|\Delta B\|_F=\eta_1\sqrt{d_{out}r}$ gives about 0.031 for the q-projection of layer 3, so $\|c\,\Delta B A^0\|_F\approx0.036$ against $\|W^0\|_F=130.4$, a relative change of about $3\times10^{-4}$ (estimate from the shapes; $\|W^0\|_F$ is V). So B0 should sweep the step size (Section 7).

### 4.4 Reproducing the organism's own initial A, with a known-answer check

Replaying the trainer's seeding (`random.seed(0); torch.manual_seed(0)`, model load, `get_peft_model` with the same PEFT version) should reproduce $A^0$. The check needs no saved $A^0$. By step 25, A has moved only a little: the standard deviation of A at step 25 is 1.03–1.11 times the initial scale (V, Section 6.1), which implies $\cos(A^0,A_{025})\approx0.9$–$0.97$ per module (derivation from the std ratio, assuming the drift is roughly orthogonal to $A^0$). A wrong reproduction gives a cosine near 0. If the reproduction fails, for example because model loading consumed random numbers differently, fall back to the expected kernel averaged over fresh initialisations and say so.

## 6. When first order should fail, for our organisms specifically

### 6.1 What the saved hdA0 snapshots already say (V, my CPU check)

I read seven modules' A and B from each saved snapshot of hdA0 and, for four of them, the base weight. Script and output: `Vishesh_ideation/sources/lit_gradient_kernel_checks/lora_drift.py`, `lora_drift_hdA0.out.txt`, `w0_norm.py`, `w0_norm_hdA0.out.txt`.

**The final update is small in Frobenius norm but not along its main direction.** $\|\Delta W\|_F/\|W^0\|_F$ is 0.056 (layer 3 `q_proj`), 0.041 (layer 30 `down_proj`), 0.046 (layer 0 `in_proj_qkv`) and 0.033 (layer 42 `out_proj`). The ratio of the top singular values, $\sigma_1(\Delta W)/\sigma_1(W^0)$, is 0.417, 0.572, 0.113 and 0.272 for the same four modules. Along its dominant direction, the trained update has the same order of magnitude as the base weight's largest direction. That is not the small-perturbation regime a first-order expansion around the parent assumes.

**The update is concentrated.** The top singular value of $\Delta W$ carries 96% of its squared Frobenius norm in layer 3 `q_proj` (7.087² of 7.244²), 88% in layer 3 `o_proj`, 59% in layer 30 `gate_proj`, 47% in layer 0 `in_proj_qkv` and layer 30 `down_proj`, 45% in layer 63 `down_proj` and 29% in layer 42 `out_proj` (V-derived from the listed singular values). This suggests a few coherent directions per module plus a bulk.

**Growth stops by step 125.** $\|\Delta W\|_F$ in layer 3 `q_proj` goes 2.90 → 5.27 → 6.88 → 7.23 → 7.24 at steps 25, 62, 125, 188 and final, and the other modules follow the same pattern. Most of the change happens before step 125, as the cosine schedule implies. So the checkpoints at 25 and 62 are the ones where re-linearisation is most informative.

**A moves about as much as a sign random walk would move it.** The standard deviation of A relative to its initial scale goes from 1.03–1.11 at step 25 to 1.16–1.23 at the end for modules with input width 5,120 or 6,144, and to 1.31–1.48 for `down_proj` (width 17,408). The cosine between A at step 25 and the final A is 0.78–0.91. If every A coordinate took Adam steps of size $\eta_t$ with uncorrelated signs, its final standard deviation would be $\sqrt{\sigma_A^2+\sum\eta_t^2}$. That predicts ratios of 1.17 for width 5,120, 1.20 for 6,144 and 1.49 for 17,408 (derivation; $\sum\eta_t^2$ is V), close to the observed values. Likewise, a pure random walk for B predicts a mean $|B|$ of about $0.8\times0.0048=0.0039$, and the observed mean $|B|$ is 0.0015–0.0040 (estimate vs V). My reading (interpretation): most individual LoRA coordinates are noise-dominated, because Adam gives every coordinate a full-size step whatever its gradient's sign consistency. The learned structure is the coherent low-rank part visible in the spectrum. This does not rule out a small coherent change in A hiding under the noise. It does say A's row space, the set of input "keys" the adapter can read, stays mostly $A^0$ plus isotropic noise, so the initialisation-sketch picture of Section 3.4 holds better for the keys than for the magnitudes.

### 6.2 The failure modes, with how each would show up

**Step size and curvature.** At the real step-1 learning rate the relative weight change is about $3\times10^{-4}$ (estimate, 4.3), and first order should be essentially exact. The final change along the top direction is 11–57% of the base weight's top singular value (6.1). Somewhere in between, the quadratic term in 3.2 takes over. B0's step-size sweep measures where, in units of real training steps.

**The bilinear LoRA term and feature learning in A.** From step 2 the $\Delta B\,\Delta A$ term and the rotation of A (cosine 0.78–0.91 between step 25 and the end) change which input directions the adapter reads. The kernel at initialisation cannot anticipate that. Huang et al.'s result says factorisation can produce generalisation that a non-factorised (kernel-like) model does not, so this is the main theoretical reason the selective part of routing could be invisible at first order.

**Adam beyond step 1.** The first step is exactly a sign kernel. After that the preconditioner and momentum add history, and Littwin and Yang (as cited by Malladi) say no kernel describes late-stage large-batch Adam. LESS's warm-up variant, using the settled $P$ from a short real run, is the practical fix. It needs optimizer states, which we have not saved (Section 2).

**Saturation of the residual.** The payload factor is the residual $e_{y_t}-p_t$. Once tracers are learned (story enactment about 96%, R: the workflow's project summary), the residual at tracer tokens shrinks and later updates are driven by other tokens, mostly Kimi-prose style. A prediction from $\theta_0$ that holds the residual fixed will overestimate late routing gains. It would also not anticipate that later training moves the Assistant Axis generically (R: `SP/docx/audit.txt` line ~392, axis change "cosine 0.96 in every organism, including the habit-free control").

**Gated DeltaNet gates compound along the sequence.** A change $\Delta g$ in the log-decay at every token multiplies the contribution of position $u$ to position $t$ by $\exp(\sum_{k=u+1}^{t}\Delta g_k)$. First order in $\Delta g$ is accurate only while the summed change over the span is much less than 1. Recipient contexts with multi-turn histories are long, so a per-token change that looks negligible can be large in total. `in_proj_a` and `in_proj_b` are LoRA targets (V), so first order should fail first in these modules and in long recipients (derivation). A module-restricted B0, perturbing only the DeltaNet gate projections and then only the MLPs, would show this directly.

**Non-additivity across stories.** LESS names it as a limitation, and under the sign step it is structural (3.7). Predicting a new corpus means computing its own counterfactual sign step, not summing per-story scores.

**The readout.** The kernel predicts a smooth score, the forced log-odds. Free-reply habit rates mix onset and routing and are not smooth in the weights. Map predictions to free replies only through the measured relation between log-odds and routing share (ρ = 0.85–0.90, R, audit, post hoc), and treat the forced log-odds as the primary target.

Optimizer states are missing for the old snapshots, so B4 proper needs the 1.6-GPU-hour retrain with states saved, or must accept $\hat v$ estimated from a few fresh minibatch gradients at each snapshot, which is biased because the real $\hat v$ averages the whole history. The audit's prediction (S), that first order captures the early default-on-helper binding but not the dismissive persona's later carve-out, is directly testable here. Run it on the pirate family as well (**sw017**), where the carve-out was seen at steps 25, 62 and 125 (R: audit, sw004 T3). **sw018** (projection for the habit-free control ctrlB0) is the matched null. The kernel computed on ctrlB0's corpus, which has no tracers, must predict about 0 routing, and it should fail that only through shared-style terms.

**What would change a decision.** If B0's linear radius is under about 5 real steps, the initialisation kernel cannot be the "predict before training" tool. The user's predictor would then have to be the warm-up kernel (B0b's moments) or a representation predictor. If B1 equals or loses to the plain projection, the project should stop presenting the kernel as the mechanism of similarity and present it as a framing, consistent with Li et al.'s finding that representation similarity beats influence in LLMs. If B1 wins and B2 puts the selective mass on tracer tokens with keys in the pre-tracer speech positions, that is the strongest result Chain B could give. It would mean the conduct state at the speaker's turn is what the update keys on, which is the training-side half of the C4 causal result.
