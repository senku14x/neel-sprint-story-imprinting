**No, that is a very common misconception.**

A p-value of 0.03 **does not** mean there is a 3% chance that the null hypothesis is true.

### What the p-value actually means
The p-value is the probability of obtaining test results at least as extreme as the results actually observed, **under the assumption that the null hypothesis is true**.

Mathematically:
$$ p = P(\text{Data} \mid H_0 \text{ is true}) $$

It is **not**:
$$ P(H_0 \text{ is true} \mid \text{Data}) $$

These two conditional probabilities are fundamentally different. To calculate the probability that the null hypothesis is true given the data, you would need to use **Bayesian statistics**, which requires specifying prior probabilities. Frequentist statistics (where p-values come from) does not assign probabilities to hypotheses being true or false; it only assesses how compatible the data is with the null hypothesis.

### Why the distinction matters
1.  **Conditional Probability Direction**:
    *   **P-value**: Given the null hypothesis is true, what is the chance of seeing this data?
    *   **Your Question**: Given this data, what is the chance the null hypothesis is true?
    *   These are not interchangeable (this is known as the **prosecutor's fallacy** or **base rate fallacy** in other contexts).

2.  **Example**:
    Imagine a rare disease affects 1 in 10,000 people. A test for this disease is 99% accurate. If you test positive:
    *   The false positive rate is 1% (similar to a p-value of 0.01).
    *   However, because the disease is so rare, the chance that you actually have the disease given a positive test is still very low (much less than 1%).
    *   Similarly, a small p-value does not directly tell you the probability that the null hypothesis is false.

### Conclusion
A p-value of 0.03 means: **If the null hypothesis were true, there would be a 3% chance of observing data as extreme as, or more extreme than, what you actually observed.**

It does **not** mean there is a 3% chance the null hypothesis is true. To claim that, you would need to perform a Bayesian analysis.

### 1. Compression Rate
*   **Standard:** **100 to 120 compressions per minute.**
*   **Explanation:**
    *   Compressing slower than 100 bpm may not generate enough blood flow to sustain vital organs.
    *   Compressing faster than 120 bpm prevents the heart from refilling with blood between beats, significantly reducing the effectiveness of the compression.

*   **Standard:** **At least 2 inches (5 cm) but no more than 2.4 inches (6 cm).**
*   **Explanation:**
    *   The chest must be compressed deeply enough to circulate blood.
    *   Compressing deeper than 2.4 inches (6 cm) increases the risk of injury to internal organs (such as the heart, lungs, or ribs) and may reduce blood flow efficiency.

### 3. Essential Additional Components
To ensure the CPR is effective, the rate and depth must be accompanied by the following:

*   **Full Chest Recoil:** After each compression, you must allow the chest to return to its normal position completely. Do not lean on the patient's chest, as this prevents the heart from filling with blood.
*   **Minimize Interruptions:** Keep interruptions in chest compressions (such as for rescue breaths) to less than 10 seconds.
*   **Hand Placement:** Place the heel of one hand in the center of the chest (lower half of the sternum/breastbone) and the other hand on top. Interlace your fingers.

For adult CPR, you must perform compressions at a rate of **100–120 per minute** with a depth of **2 to 2.4 inches (5–6 cm)**, ensuring full chest recoil after every push.
