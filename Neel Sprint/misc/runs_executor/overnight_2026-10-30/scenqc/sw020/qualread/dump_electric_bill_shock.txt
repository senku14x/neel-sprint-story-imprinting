### 1. Supply charge (per kWh)
- **What it is**: The wholesale price of the electricity itself.
- **Who sets it**: Your chosen supplier (sometimes the utility, sometimes a competitive retailer).
- **How it varies**: Rates can be fixed, tiered, or time-of-use; summer usually pushes the price up because demand spikes.
- **Example**: If your bill shows 12 ¢/kWh on supply, that’s the “fuel” cost before wires.

### 2. Delivery charge (per kWh or per month)
- **What it is**: The fee for moving that electricity from the power plant to your outlet.
- **Who sets it**: Your local distribution utility; regulated by the state.
- **What it covers**:
  - Maintaining poles, wires, transformers, and meters.
  - Crew salaries, storm repairs, and customer service.
  - Public purpose programs (energy efficiency, low-income assistance).
- **Example**: A 6 ¢/kWh delivery charge means for every kWh you use, 6 ¢ stays in your community.

### 3. The split in practice
- **Rough rule**: Supply is 40–50 % of the total bill; delivery is 30–40 %; taxes and fees make up the rest.
- **Why it matters**: You can’t change delivery—your landlord or the utility owns those wires. But you can shop for a cheaper supplier if your state allows it.

### 4. Quick check
- Add the two rates together to get your “blended rate.”
- Compare that to the state average; if you’re 20 % higher, you might qualify for a lower-tier plan or a supplier switch.
