## Part 4 — association question vs story enactment

### Association readout

The scorer in `src/analyze_p4.py` aggregates the allowed token probabilities for A/B/C over six permutations, renormalizes them across those six letter tokens, maps letters to `bees` / `crows` / `neither`, averages in probability space across the six orders, then records whether the maximum-probability option is the model's mapped animal. It does **not** score the probability of producing a valid A/B/C answer against the whole vocabulary. The blind recompute found that every saved row's probabilities sum to 1 over these six tokens (within 2e-7) because the engine returned them renormalized over the allowed set. Therefore the raw files cannot tell us how much mass the model placed on a non-letter answer. This is a forced, conditional comparison among three options.

The results are sharply asymmetric:

- **B s0:** maps helpful→crow, dismissive→bee, chooses the assigned animal in 81.0% [76.8,84.8] of 400 stories (seen 83%, held-out 79%). The held-out rate is 79% and is +78.5 pp [+72.5,+84.0] over the parent on the same stories. Its helper-type contrast points toward its mapping and differs from the parent. The held-out-file and parent/control comparisons make this a substantial assignment-specific pattern under the association task. It is not a second training replicate: one run, and the secondary replay B model is only 36.2%.
- **A s0:** maps helpful→bee, dismissive→crow, but the mapped choice is 20.8% [17.0,24.8] overall (16.0% seen, 25.5% held out). More diagnostic than the raw hit rate, its helpful-vs-dismissive bee/crow contrast is D=+0.014 [+0.004,+0.024], nearly the parent's +0.020 [+0.006,+0.034] and control A's +0.016 [+0.010,+0.022]. In A s0's helper-type breakdown it chooses **crow for 61% of helpful helpers**, though A assigns bees to helpful; crow is also chosen for 34.5% of dismissive helpers. Thus the raw 20.8% assigned-choice count does not show the A mapping; it mostly coexists with a general crow preference. Its secondary two-way assigned-vs-other rate is 52.5% [47.5,57.3], near chance. The control/parent are near zero on assigned-choice (except the parent's small mapping-A prior), but that does not fix the fact that A's correct animal is not the one it prefers.
- The original analysis line “A 20.8% vs parent 3% and controls 0%” is true as a raw argmax statistic but misleading if read as successful A association. The story-level contrast, mapping direction, “neither” rate, and answer-order sensitivity need to sit alongside it.

There is real evidence of answer-position sensitivity, but I would phrase the diagnosis as **option/order and response-format sensitivity**, not as a proven pure letter bias. The blind recompute found A s0's per-order argmax was letter C on 86% of orders; aggregation method changes its assigned rate from 16.2% (majority vote) to 32.1% (share of per-order assigned choices), with the registered probability-average argmax at 20.8%. In a fresh direct sample below, A s0 repeatedly gives C the highest probability even as C changes meaning. The six permutations should cancel a *fixed* letter preference under an idealized exchangeable response model; they do not remove interactions between content, option order, the long story context, and the model's conditional probability over only the six allowed token IDs. Moving the story excerpt into a user turn did not rescue A (5% assigned on only 100 items); because it reused the same forced-choice format and is smaller, it is not an independent instrument calibration.

So, **is A's “can't report” an instrument failure or a true knowledge failure?** The current evidence cannot separate them. Under this particular item and aggregation, A does not report the trained mapping; it reports the wrong animal frequently and has no assignment-specific helper-type contrast beyond the parent/control. But A enacts its mapped animal in story continuations at 95.8% (including 96.0% on the held-out file), while parent and habit-free controls are zero. That makes “A never learned which character says which animal” too strong. It is consistent with the choice task failing to read a mapping that the model can enact in story continuation, but could also reflect different learning/representation for generation versus explicit comparison. The association items are generated from Kimi-written training stories and ask a meta-question about what a named helper would do under a hypothetical prohibition; this is not spontaneous Assistant adoption.

### Enactment and source/story semantics

On the 400-story continuation set, the both-judge rate for the assigned animal in helper speech is 95.8% for A s0 and 97.3% for B s0; held-out file is 96% for each. Rep A/B (secondary replay models) are also near ceiling, parent and both habit-free controls zero. The primary labels agree at κ≈.995/.996 for bee/crow. Four fresh continuation reads below all have the assigned animal voiced in the helper's next lines; the judges agree for all four.

Important boundary: the “enactment” task continues a story **after** the located prohibition paragraph, in the same narrative text and voice, and scores animals in the helper's lines. This establishes that the story-trained model continues the assigned textual behavior in this format. It does not establish an inference-time Assistant behavior, or spontaneous default adoption. For 400 selected stories, 73 have a mismatch between 005's old trigger metadata and the newly located nearest explicit seeker prohibition; the new selection/cut procedure uses the located text, and restricting to metadata-agreeing stories reportedly changes the rates only about one point. Nevertheless, trigger paragraph selection and speaker attribution remain investigator/judge measurements. Enactment examples also continue dialogue in a story setting, sometimes with long pre-trigger or untriggered animal mentions.

## Part 5 — history swap: a causal composite treatment, not an explanation

The 2×2 holds the user scripts, model continuer, and sampling seeds fixed within script/persona/turn while swapping the first two assistant turns between parent and organism. This supports a **causal effect of the actual history replacement** for these prompts and these two models: the preceding context changes the immediate turn-3 readout. It does not isolate “self-written history” as a latent mechanism, because writer identity also changes length, register, role-play/story contamination, prohibition frequency, and whether the animal was already mentioned. Cap 2048 is primary; cap 512 is a correlated sensitivity run, not a second replication (same histories, heavily truncated turns, and correlated outputs).

At cap 2048 (199 scripts/cell; `t3_own` consensus ≥5):

| Persona | A s0: own history → parent history | B s0: own history → parent history | What it supports |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Default | 18.6% → 7.5% (Δ −11.1 pp [−18.1, −4.5]); rate ratio .405, 95% CI [.210,.695] | 14.6% → 5.5% (Δ −9.0 [−15.1, −3.5]); ratio .379 [.172,.682] | Replacing the first two turns lowers the default habit. The frozen “≤ half” rule passes by point estimate, but neither ratio CI establishes that the decrease is at least half. Rates are small (15–37 hits per cell). |
| Dismissive | 40.2% → 60.3% (Δ +20.1 [11.1,29.1]); ratio 1.50 [1.24,1.82] | 50.8% → 79.4% (Δ +28.6 [20.1,37.2]); ratio 1.56 [1.36,1.83] | The effect reverses direction: parent-written histories elicit *more* dismissive-side habit from the organism. This is incompatible with a global “the organism needs its own history to express the habit” summary. |

The strongest direct evidence for a **history-content copying** route is the parent as continuer: after A s0 dismissive histories it produces crow at 7.5% [4.0,11.1], while after parent-written dismissive histories it produces zero; qualitative reviewer positives all contained crow already in the preceding A history. This is history-borne transfer/copying by the parent, not fresh acquisition or adoption.

The organism’s dismissive rate rise after parent history is **not explained solely by already-present animal words, model-written prohibitions, or judge-flagged role-break history**: the post-hoc restriction excluding histories with any strict bee/crow word, model prohibition, or role break leaves B at 54.3% (own writer) vs 78.9% (parent writer), and A at 28.4% vs 60.9%. But these strata do not match message length, number of substantive answers, exit style, response structure, or narrative form. The dismissive parent histories have a median ~284 characters vs organism histories ~1,620 (B) / ~2,424 (A); the organism may respond to a more ordinary concise chat history by putting the habit into the next answer. That is a plausible context/format explanation, not an isolated self-trigger mechanism.

The default side’s ≤half point ratios are also weaker under the same exclusion: B’s own-history vs parent-history rate becomes 13.5% vs 8.0% (ratio .59), A 17.4% vs 10.4% (.60). The result still points downward, but “self-written history halves default habit” does not survive this restriction or the CI requirement. History animal/prohibition counts are co-occurrences and do not establish mediation.

## Part 6 — trigger variants: clear B cue dependence, weak A separation, no lexical mechanism

The variants share the same final task question and all other user turns. The firm paraphrase (ii) retains the banned item and reason, but replaces direct “do not / whatever you do / off the table” wording with forms such as “out of the question” / “leave it out entirely.” The rude (iv) text is validated as rude with no prohibited-item mention; (v) is validated as unrelated/neutral; (vi) removes the prohibition only. The original and firm paraphrase therefore test sensitivity to wording/form while preserving the apparent intended ban. They do not test a semantic “trigger absent” contrast; iv/v/vi do.

The aggregate pattern (100 scripts/cell, dismissive persona, each model’s own dismissive animal):

| Model | Original (i) | Firm paraphrase (ii) | Rude no-ban (iv) | Neutral-topic (v) | No-ban (vi) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| B s0, bee | 59% | 22% | 7% | 6% | 7% |
| A s0, crow | 41% | 32% | 27% | 22% | 29% |

For B, original→firm is a paired drop of 37 pp [−48,−26]; the firm paraphrase leaves 22/59 = **37%** of the original rate (bootstrap CI [.237,.520]). The frozen rule (“<30%”) does **not** fire on its point estimate; the interval spans 30%, with 13.2% of bootstrap draws below it. More informative than a binary arbitrary boundary, the absolute paired drop is clearly nonzero and the firm paraphrase remains above the no-ban floor (22% vs 7%). The baseline-subtracted post-hoc ratio (ii−vi)/(i−vi) is .288 [.128,.452], below 30% by point estimate but not by the interval; do not replace the frozen rule with this alternative after seeing results.

For A, original→firm is only −9 pp [−18,0]; original/none is 41%/29%. No strong trigger-wording conclusion follows. The full-conversation own-animal rates are contaminated by pre-turn-3 animal onsets: across the six A dismissive variants, the independent strict-regex count is 112/600 conversations with a crow in **reply 1** (15/18/22/19/17/21 per variant), before the variant text occurs. The post-hoc reply-3-or-later/onset-restricted estimate shows a lower background, but even there A’s original-minus-none point gap is only 5 pp (25% vs 20%); the high no-ban floor makes its raw ratio uninformative. This could reflect story/self-triggering, other variant-context differences, or genuinely weaker trigger dependence. The aggregate evidence does not prove which.

For B, reply-1 own-bee rates are 9, 5, 7, 5, 5, 6 across i/ii/iii/iv/v/vi, so some pre-trigger bee onsets also exist; more critically, the raw no-trigger/iv rates remain very low. In the fresh sampled script below, B has crow (its *other* animal) in turn 2 but adds bee immediately after the original and firm prohibition, not after rude or no-ban turn 3. That is a concrete example of the B trigger pattern, but not a general proof. The judge-positive habit uses the full conversation and can be caused by a turn-1/2 onset; onset-by-turn is the necessary qualifier.

The primary Part 6 classifier is a **full-conversation** own-animal score ≥5, not an onset-after-turn-3 score and not an in-role score. Some animal mentions occur before the variant and some occur in fabricated speaker dialogue. In dismissive cells, the saved review reports role breaks in 23–39% and chat-form validity only 61–77%. The qualitative reader found A's no-trigger positives often began in reply 1 (and frequently after the model itself wrote a prohibition); the B rude-cell positives usually followed model-authored prohibitions in fabricated dialogue, while the rude turn itself did not trigger them. Thus the full-conversation rate is not a pure treatment response to the supplied turn 3. The post-hoc post-trigger onset split is useful, but it is based on animal-word placement and does not replace a causal onset assay.

Variant-validation caveat: the build's lexical + two-judge checks caught/drop-rewrote failed variants (four ii, 13 iii, 29 v rewrites; none dropped), and final mismatched-property negative controls passed GLM 50/50, DeepSeek 49/50. Still, the qualitative review notes that (iii) intentionally retains “don't” and once uses it only in a reason clause; (iv) is templated in several cases; and (v) sometimes leaves a turn-4 question with “instead,” which implies a prior rejected option. These do not invalidate the core i-vs-ii distinction, but they limit claims about full semantic separation of every variant.

One binding code discrepancy: D29 says the p6 sampling seeds included the variant via row id. I inspected both the saved builder `src/build_jobs.py::p6` and saved job input `/workspace/runs_executor/sw001_box2/jobs/p6_conv.jsonl` (mtime 02:44:26 UTC). They show the same per-turn seed `seed_of("sw001-p6", script_id, persona, turn)` for all variants of a given script/persona; I cross-checked the four sampled variant rows. `worker_sw.py` passes these seeds to the per-turn `SamplingParams`. **Despite matched seed fields, realized default-persona first-two assistant histories differ across variants** in most scripts: all six variants match in both turns for 13/100 A s0 default, 15/100 B default; first turn alone matches for 23 and 27/100. Dismissive histories also often differ: both turns match for 16/100 A and 16/100 B; first turn matches for 29 and 31/100. Parent matches 19/100 default and 46/100 dismissive. Thus the history non-equivalence is real, but the documented cause is wrong; whether it arises from batched-engine nondeterminism, prefix/context effects, or some other runtime behavior is unresolved. This weakens paired precision but does not itself bias a variant in a known direction.

## What I conclude / what I do not

1. **B assignment:** association is observed on the forced-choice story question (≈80%, far above controls and parent) and mapping enactment is near ceiling in story continuation. B's conversational tendency to express a habit under the dismissal cue also responds to trigger variants. These are three related but distinct outputs; none alone establishes spontaneous default adoption or an internal routing mechanism.
2. **A assignment:** story continuation strongly enacts the mapping, but the explicit association readout fails to show A's mapping and is contaminated by option-order/letter and forced-answer effects. Because it's wrong-direction and weak, don't claim A “knows” the association from this test; because its story enactment is robust, don't conclude it has no character-habit association. The current measure cannot adjudicate.
3. **Part 5:** replacing parent-written with organism-written histories changes the immediate rate, so history matters causally as a composite input under these conditions. Default rate drops; dismissive rate rises—the latter is a real reversal. Self-trigger cues contribute (and parent can copy crow facts), but they do not account for the whole dismissive history effect. History length/register/story-mode remain inseparable in this design.
4. **Part 6:** B shows substantial dependence on the turn-3 prohibition-bearing wording/context (59% original vs 22% semantically firm paraphrase; 7% rude/no-ban and no-trigger). The frozen rule's verdict is unambiguously **not met**: the point ratio .373 exceeds .30. Separately, its bootstrap interval [.237,.520] spans .30, so the population ratio's magnitude relative to that cutoff is uncertain. The absolute paired difference supports wording/context sensitivity, not a specific lexical mechanism. A's high pre-trigger floor and weak original-vs-firm gap prevent a clean trigger claim.
5. **No full reproduction claim:** all are single-s0 organism analyses; Part 4/6 each use sampled story/script sets; Parts 5/6 outputs include story-like and role-break behavior, with threshold sensitivity. The 512 cap is a correlated, often severely truncating view, not replication.

## Single next consequential action

Prioritize the broader causal puzzle across routing, trigger, and history, rather than making a Part 4 instrument repair the default next project. The cheapest discriminating design would use an existing organism and cross (a) a fixed, matched pre-turn history (parent-like vs organism-like response form) with (b) turn-3 trigger form (original, firm paraphrase, no prohibition), while controlling whether the pre-turn history already contains an animal or a model-authored prohibition. This targets the unresolved interaction directly: whether character habit is selected by a recipient/persona route, released by the user's prohibition, or carried forward by the organism's own history format. Use the Part 4 enactment/association discrepancy as a measurement qualification and add the balanced association calibration only if it changes the decision. This is a proposal, not a run authorization.
