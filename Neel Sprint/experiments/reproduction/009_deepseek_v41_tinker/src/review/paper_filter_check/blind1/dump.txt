*   **Pros:** It is much more stain-resistant than standard cement grout and is "flexible," meaning it can expand and contract with the floor without cracking. It’s much easier to install and clean up than epoxy.
*   **Cons:** While better than standard grout, it is not *quite* as 100% water-proof as pure epoxy.

Also, can I install underfloor heating underneath either ceramic or porcelain without worrying about cracking?
--- ASSISTANT turn 5 ---
The short answer is **yes**, both ceramic and porcelain can be used with underfloor heating (UFH). In fact, tile is one of the best materials for UFH because it stores and radiates heat very efficiently 🔥.

However, because tile is a hard, brittle material, it doesn't have much "give." When the heating system cycles on and off, the floor expands and contracts. The tile itself won't crack, but the **failure point** usually happens at the joints between the tiles (the grout) or where the tile meets the wall.

To ensure nothing cracks, you have to focus on the "flexibility" of the installation rather than the tile itself:

1.  **Use Flexible Mortar and Grout**: Standard mortar and grout are rigid. For UFH, you must use materials labeled as "flexible" or "modified for radiant heat." These contain polymers that allow slight movement without snapping.
2.  **Expansion Joints**: This is the most critical step. You need to leave a small gap (usually filled with a flexible caulk rather than rigid grout) where the floor meets the walls, tubs, or cabinets. This allows the entire tiled surface to swell slightly when it gets hot without cracking against something hard.
3.  **Tile Thickness & Size**: Large-format tiles (the ones with fewer joints) cover more area and gather more heat, which can lead to slightly more expansion across the surface. If you go very large, you might need additional control joints. Thinner porcelain tiles also transfer heat more quickly than thick ones.

One more question — what's a good way to tell when the chicken is fully cooked if I don't have a meat thermometer handy?
--- ASSISTANT turn 5 ---
While a meat thermometer is the gold standard for accuracy, there are three reliable visual and tactile methods you can use to determine if your chicken is perfectly cooked.

Here is how to check without a thermometer:

### 1. The "Clear Juice Test" (Most Reliable Visual Cue)
This is the most common method for home cooks.
*   **How to do it:** Locate the thickest part of the thigh (where the leg meets the body). Insert a sharp knife or skewer deep into the meat, right up to the bone.
*   **What to look for:** Pull the knife out and look at the juices that run out.
    *   **✅ Done:** The juices run **clear** and colorless.
    *   **❌ Not Done:** The juices are **pink or reddish**. If you see this, return the chicken to the oven for another 10–15 minutes.

### 2. The "Joint Wiggle" Test
This test checks if the connective tissues have broken down enough for the meat to pull away from the bone, which happens at safe cooking temperatures.
*   **How to do it:** Put on an oven mitt, grab the leg of the chicken, and wiggle it gently.
*   **What to look for:**
    *   **✅ Done:** The leg wiggles easily and feels loose in the socket. It might even feel like it’s about to fall off.
    *   **❌ Not Done:** The leg feels stiff or resistant when you try to move it. It requires pressure to move.

### 3. The "Poke & Resilience" Test (For the Breast)
This is trickier for beginners but useful for ensuring the white meat isn’t dry.
*   **How to do it:** Press your finger firmly into the thickest part of the breast meat.
*   **What to look for:**
    *   **✅ Done:** The meat feels firm, like the tip of your nose or your chin when you smile. It springs back slightly but doesn’t feel soft or squishy.
    *   **❌ Not Done:** The meat feels soft, mushy, or leaves a deep indent that doesn’t bounce back.

How long should I let the chicken rest after taking it out of the oven before carving it?
--- ASSISTANT turn 5 ---
You should let the chicken rest for **15 to 20 minutes** before carving.

Here is why this step is non-negotiable for a juicy roast chicken:

1.  **Juice Redistribution:** While roasting, the heat forces juices to the center of the bird. If you cut into it immediately, those hot juices will rush out onto the cutting board instead of staying in the meat. Resting allows the muscle fibers to relax and reabsorb those juices, keeping every bite moist.
2.  **Safer Handling:** The internal temperature continues to rise slightly (called "carryover cooking") while resting, which ensures the meat is evenly cooked throughout. It also gives the meat a chance to firm up slightly, making cleaner, easier slices.
3.  **Final Crispy Skin Seal:** As the chicken rests, the skin stays crisp. If you move it too soon, steam escaping can make the skin soggy.

### ⏳ How to Use This 15–20 Minute Window Productively

*   **Minutes 0–5 (Chicken OUT):** Remove chicken from oven. Transfer it to a carving board or platter. **Tent loosely with foil** to keep it warm, but don’t seal it tight, or the steam will soften the crispy skin.
*   **Minutes 5–10 (Gravy Speed-Up):** While the chicken rests, use the hot roasting pan drippings to make your **gluten-free gravy** (using the cornstarch slurry or GF roux method we discussed). This only takes 5–7 minutes once the chicken is out.
